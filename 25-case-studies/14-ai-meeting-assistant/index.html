    AI Meeting Assistant — System Design Case Study¶

    This case study is written from the perspective of a platform team building a production AI meeting assistant — something in the shape of Otter.ai, Fireflies.ai, Grain, or Microsoft Copilot for Teams. The product joins video calls, listens, and turns an hour of conversation into artifacts a person who missed the meeting can act on in three minutes.

    The design thesis that drives every decision in this chapter: a meeting assistant that only transcribes is a transcription service, not an assistant. Transcription is a commodity — every ASR vendor sells it. The commercial value this product exists to create is the structured extraction layer on top: action items with identified owners and deadlines, decisions with context and rationale, topic-segmented summaries that save a reader the full hour. Every architectural choice below — the two-phase pipeline, the double diarization pass, the four-stage LLM extraction, the integration push layer — exists in service of that structured output. Transcription quality matters because poor transcription degrades extraction quality, not for its own sake. Diarization matters because "Alice will send the report by Friday" is an action item with an owner only if the system knows Alice said it; "Speaker 2 will send the report by Friday" is nearly worthless to whoever reads the notes next week.

    This chapter shares its ASR and diarization infrastructure with the AI Voice Agent case study, and the contrast between the two is instructive precisely because the low-level components look similar while the systems built from them are almost opposites. The voice agent processes bidirectional conversation under a ~700ms perceptual latency threshold and produces a spoken response. The meeting assistant passively listens to multi-speaker audio with no real-time response requirement at all, and produces structured documents. That contrast is called out explicitly at every layer below where it matters — latency, diarization, and model selection most of all.

    Requirements¶

    Functional — two processing paths and five output types. The two-path split is the load-bearing structural decision in this system, so it comes before anything else:

    • Processing Path 1 — real-time (during the meeting). A bot joins the live meeting — either through a native conferencing-platform integration or by joining as a browser participant — and produces a rolling transcript with speaker labels that updates within 5 seconds of each utterance, plus a running "so far" summary refreshed every 5 minutes. This path runs streaming ASR and in-progress diarization, both meaningfully less accurate than their offline counterparts, because the value here is availability during the meeting, not archival quality. A participant can glance at who said what a minute ago, or search the in-progress transcript mid-meeting, without waiting for anything to finish.
    • Processing Path 2 — post-meeting (within 5 minutes of meeting end). The system reprocesses the complete recorded audio with higher-accuracy offline models: diarization and ASR both re-run, speaker identities resolve to real participant names, and the corrected full transcript passes through a long-context LLM pipeline for structured extraction. The 5-minute SLO exists so a participant gets their notes before they've left their desk for the next meeting — the moment the value of "what did we just decide" decays fastest.
    • Output Type 1 — verbatim transcript with speaker attribution and timestamps. [Speaker Name] [HH:MM:SS]: [utterance], names resolved from the calendar participant list, voice enrollment, or self-introduction detection, with a confidence score on every speaker attribution so low-confidence segments can be flagged for user review rather than silently presented as fact.
    • Output Type 2 — topic-segmented summary. The meeting divides into N automatically detected topic blocks, each with an auto-generated title and a 2–4 sentence summary of what was discussed, decided, and any outcomes — letting a reader jump straight to the 8 minutes of the meeting that concern them instead of reading a 45-minute transcript top to bottom.
    • Output Type 3 — action items, the highest-value output the product produces. A structured list of commitments, each with an owner (who committed), a task (what they committed to), a deadline (explicit or inferred), and context (the supporting transcript excerpt). An action item is distinguished from a mere suggestion by the presence of a first-person commitment ("I'll have that by Friday") or an explicit assignment ("Alice, can you handle that?") — ambiguous cases are still surfaced, at a lower confidence score, rather than silently dropped.
    • Output Type 4 — decision log. What was decided, what alternatives were considered, and who made the call. A decision is a resolution of a deliberation; an action item is a follow-up task that may or may not result from one — the two are extracted separately because conflating them loses information (a decision with no action item is still worth recording, and vice versa).
    • Output Type 5 — integration push. Action items become tasks in connected trackers (Jira, Asana, Linear, Notion, Todoist); the summary attaches to the calendar event as a note; for sales meetings, the summary posts to the connected CRM opportunity (Salesforce, HubSpot). Every push is previewed to the user before it fires — the platform never writes to a third-party system without explicit confirmation.

    Data retention Configurable per org: ephemeral (transcript deleted after notes generate) to 7-year (compliance) Default: 1-year transcript retention, audio deleted immediately after post-meeting processing
    Recording consent Bot announces itself at meeting start; announcement is architecturally non-skippable Silent recording is never permitted at the product level, not just the policy level
    Privacy opt-out Any participant can opt out per meeting; their audio isn't captured and their voice is masked in the shared transcript —

    Explicitly out of scope: real-time translation (translating the meeting into another language live — a distinct product surface); meeting scheduling (finding a time, sending invites — a separate AI-scheduler problem); video-based sentiment or facial-expression analysis (out of scope and ethically fraught enough that it doesn't belong in this platform's surface area at all).

    Speaker diarization, notably, is in scope here after being explicitly named out of scope in the AI Voice Agent chapter — that chapter defers multi-speaker distinction to "a different case study," and this is that case study. A one-on-one phone call doesn't need to know who's speaking; a five-person meeting is unusable without it.

    Capacity Planning¶

    Model this at a scale comparable to Otter.ai's or Fireflies.ai's platform footprint, using the method from GPU Sizing & Capacity Planning:

    Step Assumption Result
    Enterprise users 100,000 100K users
    Meetings/user/day 3 (typical knowledge-worker load) 300,000 meetings/day
    Average meeting duration 45 minutes —
    Total audio-hours/day 300,000 × 45 min = 13.5M minutes 225,000 audio-hours/day
    Avg. participants/meeting 4 speakers 1.2M speaker-hours of distinct audio/day
    Peak concurrent meetings Meetings concentrate 9 AM–4 PM local time across US time zones, peaking ~1 PM ET: 300,000 / (12 business hrs / 0.75 avg meeting duration) ~14,000 concurrent meetings at peak
    Post-meeting processing jobs 300,000/day, triggered at meeting end ~3.5 jobs/sec average, ~12/sec peak
    LLM summarization token demand 300,000 meetings × 9,000 words/60-min transcript × 1.3 tok/word ~3.5B tokens/day
    Integration pushes 300,000 meetings × 8 action items avg × 60% with integrations enabled ~1.44M API calls/day ≈ 16.7 calls/sec

    The infrastructure profile is unlike consumer chat's high-QPS/short-session shape: moderate concurrent connection count (14,000 meetings) paired with massive sustained audio-ingest bandwidth and a large post-meeting batch workload. The dominant cost is ASR — 13.5M minutes/day, at any pricing tier, is the single largest line item and the primary build-vs-buy decision in the entire system (Cost Model returns to this directly).

    Scale Estimation¶

    1. Real-time audio ingest bandwidth. Each concurrent meeting streams audio from N participants. A mixed-stream integration (all speakers combined into one channel) at 14,000 concurrent meetings, Opus codec, 64kbps: 14,000 × 64,000 = 896 Mbps sustained ingest at peak. Per-participant streams (where the conferencing API supports them) at 4 speakers × 64kbps × 14,000 meetings: 3.58 Gbps — four times the bandwidth, but the diarization-accuracy payoff (Detailed Design, item 1) makes it the preferred mode wherever it's available.

    2. Transcript storage. 300,000 meetings/day × 45 min × 150 words/min = 2.025B words/day ≈ 14.2GB/day raw text, ~2.8GB/day gzip-compressed. At 1-year default retention: ~1TB of compressed transcript, roughly $23/month/TB in S3 — negligible. The transcript, not the audio, is the artifact retained long-term.

    3. Meeting embeddings for search. Each 45-minute meeting yields ~90 thirty-second transcript segments, each embedded for semantic search. 300,000 meetings/day × 90 segments = 27M new embeddings/day. At 1536-d float32 (6KB each): 162GB/day, ~59TB/year at full retention — the dominant storage cost in the system, by a wide margin over transcript text. Three retention options: (a) keep everything, 59TB/year; (b) keep only the last 90 days, ~14.6TB; (c) embed only meetings a user has explicitly flagged for search. Most deployments land on (b) or (c) — users rarely search meetings older than 90 days, and paying for a year of ANN-indexed vectors nobody queries is pure waste.

    4. Post-meeting processing queue. 300,000 jobs/day at ~3 min average processing time per 45-min meeting = 900,000 CPU-minutes/day = 15,000 CPU-hours/day. This batch load spreads across 24 hours globally but concentrates in US evening hours (5–10 PM ET, when the bulk of US meetings end); peak queue depth runs to roughly 20,000 concurrent post-meeting jobs in that window. A priority-routed job queue (Redis or SQS) processes shorter meetings first so more users get notes faster during the peak.

    5. Speaker enrollment database. For identity resolution beyond calendar matching, users can enroll a 30-second voice sample, stored as a 192-d float32 x-vector embedding: 100,000 users × 192 × 4 bytes = 73.7MB — trivial, cached entirely in memory across the diarization fleet.

    Six subsystems, in the order data actually flows through them.

    1. Audio ingestion and meeting bot architecture¶

    The bot is a software process that joins a video conference and receives its audio. Two integration models:

    Native conferencing-platform API (preferred). Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet all offer bot/recording APIs on enterprise plans that hand back per-participant audio streams. Per-participant streams are the diarization ideal — each stream's identity is the speaker identity, so diarization work all but disappears (Detailed Design, item 3, Method 1). The tradeoff: this tier requires enterprise-level platform permissions and may carry platform-imposed rate limits or per-minute costs.

    Browser-based bot (fallback). For platforms without a recording API, or consumer/free-tier accounts, the bot joins as a WebRTC browser session — appearing as an ordinary participant — and captures the mixed audio output of the browser's audio context. Diarization then has to run on the mixed signal in software, which is both less accurate and more compute-intensive, but it works universally across any WebRTC-based platform with no special API access required.

    The bot's first action on joining is non-negotiable: it announces itself. "This meeting is being recorded and transcribed by [Assistant Name]. If you wish to opt out, please let the meeting organizer know." Audio capture does not begin until the announcement completes or 10 seconds elapse, whichever comes first — this ordering is architecturally enforced in the bot's join sequence, not a configuration flag, precisely so a silent-recording mode can't exist even by accident.

    2. Streaming ASR and in-progress diarization — the real-time path¶

    For Path 1, streaming ASR and in-progress diarization run in parallel on the incoming audio.

    Streaming ASR is architecturally the same pattern as Chapter 11's streaming ASR — 100ms audio chunks in, partial transcripts back. The difference is the latency budget: 5 seconds here versus 700ms for the voice agent, and that 7× looser target buys room for a materially more accurate streaming model. Whisper-medium behind a streaming wrapper, or Deepgram Nova-2 streaming, both hit the 5-second target with better WER than the smallest, fastest Whisper tiers the voice agent is forced into by its latency floor.

    In-progress diarization (streaming) uses a sliding-window approach: the diarization model (pyannote.audio's streaming pipeline) holds an x-vector embedding for every speaker it's identified so far in the meeting. For each new 2-second chunk: extract an x-vector → compare by cosine similarity to known speaker embeddings → above threshold, attribute to the existing speaker; below threshold, declare a new speaker and add it to the set. This produces speaker labels within 2–3 seconds of each utterance, but at 20–30% DER — meaningfully worse than offline diarization, because the model only ever sees the preceding few seconds of context, never the whole meeting. The real-time transcript marks low-confidence speaker attributions visually (a different color in the UI) so participants can catch and correct misattributions live, while the meeting is still fresh in everyone's memory.

    Transcript assembler merges ASR output (text with timestamps) with diarization output (speaker labels with time ranges) by timestamp alignment — every word inherits the speaker label of whichever diarization segment covers its timestamp.

    3. Offline diarization and speaker identity resolution — the post-meeting path¶

    Path 2 re-runs both ASR and diarization on the complete recorded audio with offline models built for accuracy, not latency.

    Full offline diarization (pyannote.audio's offline pipeline) processes the entire meeting in one pass. Because it sees the whole recording, it estimates each speaker's embedding as a centroid averaged across all of that speaker's segments — not just the preceding 2 seconds — and runs a global optimization (spectral clustering) that minimizes total diarization error across the whole meeting rather than making a greedy local call per chunk. This is what gets DER from 20–30% down to under 10%, and it's the concrete reason the platform accepts the cost of running diarization twice: streaming produces the live view, offline produces the canonical record, and the canonical record is what every downstream extraction step depends on.

    Speaker identity resolution runs four methods in priority order:

    1. Per-participant audio streams. If the conferencing platform provided per-participant streams, identity is already known from stream identity — no diarization model needed at all, direct attribution.
    2. Voice enrollment matching. For users who've enrolled a voiceprint, compare each diarized speaker's centroid x-vector against the enrollment database by cosine similarity; a match above 0.85 resolves the identity. For a 4-speaker meeting with 3 enrolled participants, 3 speakers resolve automatically and one remains "Speaker 4."
    3. Calendar participant matching with LLM self-introduction detection. The meeting invitation supplies the participant list; the LLM scans the transcript for self-introductions ("Hi, I'm Sarah from the product team") and third-person name references ("As John mentioned earlier...") to bind speaker IDs to names. This works even with zero enrollment: if the transcript has "Sarah, can you take that?" followed by "Sure, I'll have it by Monday," the LLM infers the responder is Sarah from conversational context alone.
    4. Manual correction — the fallback. Any speaker still unresolved is shown to the meeting owner as "Unknown Speaker" with a one-click correction UI. Corrections feed back into the voice enrollment database, so the platform gets better at recognizing that same participant next time.

    4. Long-context LLM processing pipeline for structured extraction¶

    A 60-minute transcript is ~9,000 words ≈ 12,000 tokens — well inside a 128K+ context window, but naive one-shot processing ("here's the transcript, give me the action items") underperforms a structured, sequential pipeline. Four passes:

    • Pass 1 — topic segmentation. Full transcript in, one prompt: "Identify the distinct topics discussed. For each: title, start timestamp, end timestamp, one-sentence description." Output: a JSON array of topic segments. This pass needs the full 12,000-token context because topic boundaries are only visible in the whole-meeting arc.
    • Pass 2 — action item extraction, per segment. Each topic segment (typically 1,000–3,000 tokens) is extracted independently: "Extract all explicit commitments and task assignments. For each: owner, task, deadline, confidence (high/medium/low), and the exact supporting quote." Smaller context per call means higher precision — the model isn't scanning an hour of unrelated conversation for every extraction.
    • Pass 3 — decision extraction. Same per-segment structure as action items, but the prompt distinguishes a decision ("We've decided to use Postgres for the new service") from a discussion ("We talked about whether to use Postgres or MySQL"). Output includes the chosen option, rejected alternatives, and the stated rationale.
    • Pass 4 — summary generation. Each segment gets a 3–5 sentence summary; the meeting as a whole gets a one-paragraph executive summary. Segment summaries plus segment titles form the meeting notes document.

    Why four passes instead of one? A single pass asked for everything at once trades off between outputs — it under-extracts action items to make room for a better summary, or vice versa — and produces less precise topic boundaries, which require the model to hold the full transcript in view without being distracted by extraction instructions at the same time. A/B tests show the multi-pass pipeline achieving 15–25% higher action-item recall than the single-pass version, at the cost of 3–4× more LLM tokens per meeting — a trade the platform takes without hesitation once the Cost Model (below) makes clear how cheap LLM tokens are relative to ASR.

    5. Action item quality — precision, recall, and confidence calibration¶

    Action-item extraction has two failure modes with asymmetric cost: false positives (flagging a non-commitment as an action item) and false negatives (missing a real commitment). For this product, false negatives are the more damaging failure — a missed "I'll have the report by Thursday" is a dropped ball, and a user who discovers the assistant dropped it stops trusting every future meeting's notes, not just that one.

    The precision/recall balance is a configurable confidence threshold: lower it and recall rises at the cost of precision. Platform default is to surface everything at confidence ≥ 0.5, visually distinguishing high-confidence (≥ 0.8) from medium-confidence (0.5–0.8) items, with the threshold itself configurable per organization.

    The supporting quote is the trust mechanism that makes this workable: every action item ships with the verbatim transcript excerpt it was extracted from, timestamped and linked to that point in the recording. A user skeptical of an extraction clicks through to hear the original audio. This transparency is what lets users correct extractions cheaply and confidently rather than either blindly trusting or blindly distrusting the output — and every correction becomes a labeled example that feeds the extraction pipeline's ongoing evaluation set (Observability Layer).

    6. Integration push layer¶

    Executes API calls against connected third-party systems, strictly after the user previews and confirms — either a single "Push to Jira" for the full batch, or per-item selective push. No write happens on a user's behalf without that explicit confirmation step.

    • Task trackers (Jira, Asana, Linear, Notion): one API call per action item, creating a task with title, assignee (if the owner's account is connected), and due date. If the owner's account isn't connected, the task assigns to the meeting organizer with "Action item for: [Owner Name]" in the description, rather than silently dropping the assignment.
    • Calendar (Google Calendar, Outlook): the meeting summary appends to the calendar event as a note, visible to every invited participant.
    • CRM (Salesforce, HubSpot): for meetings tied to a sales opportunity (detected by matching meeting title or participant domains to CRM records), the summary posts as an activity note on that opportunity.

    Failures route through a retry queue with exponential backoff (1min, 5min, 15min, 1hr). After four failures, the user is notified with a copyable action-item list and a manual "add to Jira yourself" path — never a silent drop. Integration push never blocks meeting-notes delivery: notes reach the user regardless of whether the downstream push has succeeded, failed, or is still retrying.

    Retrieval Layer¶

    Three retrieval patterns serve three different moments in a user's relationship with their meeting history.

    Historical meeting search — the primary surface. Users ask "When did we discuss the API redesign?" or "What did Alice commit to last week?" The query embeds and runs ANN search over the pre-computed 30-second segment embeddings, filtered by date range, participants, and meeting title. Each hit returns the speaker, timestamp, segment text, and a link into the full notes. Semantic search matters here specifically because meeting language is loose — "infrastructure migration" needs to match "moving to the new cloud architecture," which keyword search would miss entirely.

    Retrieval is access-scoped: a user can only search meetings they participated in or were explicitly shared. That filter is applied at the vector-store query layer, not as a post-retrieval check — the ANN search itself only ever considers segments from meetings the requester has access to, the same retrieval-time-not-post-hoc discipline argued in the Enterprise RAG Platform's permission architecture. A document a user can't see should never enter the candidate set, let alone the response.

    Cross-meeting context for the running summary. While generating the live "so far" summary, the LLM can optionally pull relevant context from past meetings with the same participants or topic: "In the meeting on Nov 8, Alice agreed to complete the infrastructure design by Nov 15 — the current meeting is discussing the same deadline." This grounds the incremental summary in institutional history rather than treating every meeting as if it started from zero. Retrieved past-meeting context is explicitly labeled in the prompt as [Context from previous meeting: ...] so the model never confuses a past decision with a decision being made right now.

    Participant agenda retrieval, before the bot joins. When the bot is scheduled, the platform pulls the calendar event's agenda (if present) and prior notes from earlier meetings in the same recurring series. Loaded into the LLM's system prompt, this sharpens both topic detection (knowing "this is the weekly sprint planning meeting" primes the model for its recurring shape: retro, status, blockers, next-sprint planning) and action-item extraction (knowing the expected structure reduces false negatives on items that follow a familiar pattern).

    Agent Layer¶

    Agentic behavior in this system is deliberately narrow — constrained to post-meeting integration actions, never an autonomous loop during the meeting itself, which stays strictly passive listening:

    • create_task(tracker, title, assignee, due_date, description) → task_url — creates a Jira, Asana, or Linear task.
    • attach_meeting_notes(calendar_event_id, notes_markdown) → status — appends the summary to the calendar event.
    • create_crm_activity(opportunity_id, summary, action_items) → activity_id — posts a sales-call summary to a CRM opportunity.
    • send_summary_email(recipients, subject, notes_html) → message_id — emails the summary; requires explicit per-send approval, not a standing permission.

    Every tool call surfaces in a "pending actions" panel after the meeting. The user reviews the proposals — which Jira project each task lands in, which CRM opportunity to attach notes to — and clicks Confirm before anything executes. No autonomous write happens without that click, echoing the same discipline the Human-in-the-Loop Architecture chapter argues generally for state-changing actions: a tool result is something the user authorizes, not something the agent narrates as already done.

    The one place the platform makes an autonomous inference rather than waiting for a user decision is CRM-opportunity matching: when a meeting's participant email domains match a company already in the CRM, the platform proactively suggests which opportunity the meeting likely belongs to. The user confirms or picks a different one. That single-step suggestion is the outer limit of autonomy in this layer — the platform never autonomously attaches notes to an opportunity without that confirmation, the same way Tool Use Architecture draws the line between a tool the agent may call freely and one that requires a human in the loop before it fires.

    Model Layer¶

    Four model tiers, each serving a distinct pipeline stage — and this is the layer where the contrast with the AI Voice Agent is sharpest, because the two systems share ASR and diarization technology but tune it toward opposite goals.

    Tier Component Model choice Notes
    1 Streaming ASR (real-time) Deepgram Nova-2 streaming, or self-hosted Whisper-medium with a streaming wrapper Target < 5s latency, < 8% WER clear audio. Custom vocabulary (product names, team member names, jargon — the most-transcribed and most-mis-transcribed words in any meeting) reduces domain-term WER 30–50% when loaded per organization at session start
    2 Offline ASR (post-meeting) Whisper-large-v3, self-hosted, with diarization alignment At 5× real-time factor, a 60-min meeting takes 12 min serially — too slow for the SLO. Chunk the audio into 15-min segments, process 4 in parallel: 3 minutes total for a 60-min meeting
    3 Speaker diarization pyannote.audio (streaming + offline pipelines); ECAPA-TDNN x-vector embeddings (192-d) for both diarization and voice-enrollment matching GPU-bound; one A10G handles ~100 concurrent streaming-diarization sessions
    4 Long-context LLM extraction Claude 3.5 Sonnet or GPT-4o (128K context) 4-pass pipeline: full-transcript pass for topic segmentation, then 2,000–4,000-token segment passes for action items and decisions. Structured JSON enforced via function calling/structured output — free-form extraction plus a parser is too fragile in production

    The contrast with Chapter 11 in one sentence: the voice agent optimizes ASR and diarization for latency under a perceptual threshold, accepting whatever accuracy that latency budget allows; the meeting assistant optimizes for accuracy under a much looser latency budget, and then runs the entire ASR/diarization pass a second time, offline, purely to buy back the accuracy the real-time pass had to sacrifice. Same model families, opposite tuning direction, because the two products are evaluated against opposite failure modes — a voice agent that's 200ms slower is a bad product; a meeting assistant that's 200ms slower is imperceptible, but one that misattributes a commitment to the wrong person produces a wrong action item that erodes trust in every future meeting's notes.

    Model updates gate on a held-out set of manually annotated meeting transcripts (action items and decisions labeled by human annotators); a regression of more than 5% in action-item F1 triggers an automatic rollback rather than shipping and monitoring in production.

    Observability Layer¶

    • Action item recall (primary product-quality metric). Fraction of real meeting commitments captured in the structured output, measured via user feedback ("Was an action item missed?") and monthly sampled human review of ~200 meetings against annotated ground truth. Target > 85%; a drop below 80% is investigated first in the LLM extraction pass — usually a model update silently changing structured-output formatting or confidence calibration.
    • Action item precision. Fraction of extracted items that are valid commitments, measured via "this is not an action item" feedback. Target > 80% — low precision trains users to ignore the output entirely, which is worse than missing items, because it forfeits the product's entire value proposition rather than just under-delivering on it.
    • Post-meeting processing P50/P95 latency for 60-minute meetings. Target P50 3 min, P95 5 min (the SLO). A P95 regression usually traces to one of: ASR batch workers saturated beyond concurrent-job capacity, a shift in the meeting-duration distribution (more 90-minute meetings than typical), or an LLM API latency increase.
    • Speaker attribution accuracy, proxied by the rate of user corrections to speaker labels in the transcript UI. A correction rate above 10% of segments for a given organization points at either low voice-enrollment adoption or a diarization model struggling with that org's specific acoustic environment (a particular conference room with bad acoustics, say) — tracked per-organization because the fix differs by cause.
    • Real-time transcript lag P95. Utterance end to UI appearance; target < 5s, monitored with synthetic tests — a bot speaking a known sentence into a test meeting, measuring lag to the corresponding transcript update.
    • Integration push success rate, per connector. Fraction of push attempts succeeding within 3 retries. Below 95% for a specific connector (Jira, say) signals an API compatibility break or rate-limit issue, and triggers both investigation and user notification.
    • Custom vocabulary hit rate. Fraction of domain-specific terms correctly transcribed, tracked by comparing against expected vocabulary (inferred from calendar titles or participant email domains). A low hit rate usually means custom-vocabulary loading failed at session start, not that the ASR model itself regressed.
    • Meeting bot join success rate. Fraction of scheduled meetings the bot joined before start. Failures span conferencing-API errors, meeting-password issues, and cancelled/rescheduled meetings. This is the highest-impact failure mode in the whole system — a join failure means zero transcript, not a degraded one — so it carries the tightest target, > 99%.

    Security Layer¶

    Meeting content sensitivity and access control. Meetings routinely carry strategy, personnel, legal, and customer information. Access is scoped to: (a) participants confirmed via their email in the meeting invitation and the conferencing platform's own participant list; (b) the meeting organizer, who can explicitly extend access; (c) organization admins, every access by whom is audit-logged. Cross-meeting access by, say, a manager who wasn't invited is not a configuration option anywhere in the system — it's a hard enforcement at the data layer, not a policy the UI happens to also respect.

    Recording consent architecture. The consent announcement cannot be skipped by construction: the bot joins, plays the announcement, and only then does audio capture begin. If the host mutes the bot before the announcement finishes playing, capture does not start and the session is marked consent_announcement_failed — recording not started, producing an audit trail that consent was attempted and preserving the invariant that no audio is ever captured silently. For meetings with participants in jurisdictions with stricter consent regimes (Germany's two-party consent requirements, for instance), the platform supports per-organization consent-notice templates matched to local regulation.

    Audio retention and deletion. Raw audio persists only long enough to run the post-meeting pipeline — typically 2 hours after meeting end — then is deleted, with the deletion logged. This means a breach of the meeting-notes database never exposes raw audio, because by the time notes exist, the audio backing them is already gone. The transcript, retained per the org's configured policy, is the persisted sensitive artifact going forward. For organizations that need zero transcript retention (legal firms guarding privilege, for instance), an ephemeral mode generates notes from the transcript and then deletes the transcript itself, keeping only the structured notes.

    GDPR right to erasure. A meeting participant — especially a non-employee guest on a vendor call — can request deletion of their voice data and transcript segments. The pipeline: identify every session where the participant's email appears in the participant list; delete any voice-enrollment record for them; redact their attributed transcript segments to [Participant - Removed] while preserving the rest of the meeting's content for other participants. Full meeting erasure (all content, not one participant's segments) is available too, at the cost of removing every other participant's access to that meeting's notes — the same right-to-deletion mechanics discussed generally in PII & Privacy Engineering, applied here to a case where the "record" being deleted has other people's data entangled in it by nature.

    Cost Model¶

    Per 45-minute meeting:

    Cost component Driver Per-meeting cost
    Streaming ASR (real-time, Deepgram Nova-2) 45 min at $0.0059/min $0.27
    Offline ASR (post-meeting, self-hosted Whisper-large-v3) 45 min processed in 9 min across 4 GPU workers, A10G at $0.80/hr $0.48
    Speaker diarization (offline, GPU) Included in the ASR worker time above —
    LLM processing (4 passes, Claude 3 Haiku class) 12,000 input + 1,500 output tokens at $0.25/M in, $1.25/M out $0.005
    Transcript embedding for search 90 segments × 300 tokens at $0.02/M $0.0005
    Integration API calls ~8 calls, negligible per-call cost $0.01
    Storage (transcript 1yr, embeddings 90d) ~25KB transcript + ~540KB embeddings, blended storage pricing ~$0.003/meeting/month
    Total per-meeting serving cost ~$0.76

    At 300,000 meetings/day: \(0.76 × 300,000 = **\)228,000/day = $6.84M/month** in serving cost.

    Revenue at $20/user/month individual or $15/user/month enterprise, \(17 avg × 100,000 users = **\)1.7M/month — far short of \(6.84M/month in cost. The single lever that closes most of that gap: ASR (\)0.27 streaming + $0.48 offline = \(0.75/meeting) is 99% of the per-meeting serving cost. Self-hosting streaming ASR (Whisper-medium instead of managed Deepgram) drops that leg to ~\)0.02/meeting; offline ASR is already self-hosted. Total per-meeting cost falls to $0.02 + $0.48 + \(0.015 ≈ **\)0.515/meeting → \(154,500/day = **\)4.63M/month**.

    Still short of revenue — the honest conclusion is that the platform needs one or more of: higher enterprise pricing ($30–50/user/month tiers), per-meeting charges past some duration threshold, or consent-based monetization of aggregate meeting data (federated insights, cross-org benchmarking). What the arithmetic makes unambiguous is where the leverage sits: the LLM extraction pipeline costs $0.005/meeting — effectively free — while ASR costs over $0.50/meeting. Spend engineering effort improving extraction quality (better prompts, better models) freely; spend it on ASR infrastructure optimization first, because that's where a percentage-point improvement actually moves the P&L.

    Failure Handling¶

    Failure mode Detection Response
    Meeting bot fails to join (conferencing API error, bad credentials) Bot join timeout, 90s after scheduled start Notify the user immediately with a manual recording link; log the failure reason. Platform-specific join-failure rate above 5% triggers an integration health investigation for that platform
    Streaming ASR degraded (latency > 10s) Real-time transcript lag P99 alarm Fail over to a lower-latency, lower-quality ASR fallback (Whisper-small); surface "Transcript quality reduced due to service load" in the live UI. A degraded live transcript still beats none
    Post-meeting processing job fails after 3 retries Job-failure event, retries exhausted Deliver the raw transcript immediately with "structured notes are still being generated" messaging; schedule a manual retry in the next low-load window. Never leave the user with nothing
    LLM structured output malformed (JSON parse failure) Post-generation parse failure Re-run that specific pass with explicit JSON-repair instructions injected. Two more failures → fall back to free-form extraction with a "action items may need manual review" warning
    Diarization error rate high (> 20% user corrections) Per-meeting correction-rate monitoring Offer to reprocess diarization incorporating the user's corrections; feed corrected segments into the voice-enrollment database to improve future meetings with the same participants
    Integration push failure (task-tracker API timeout) HTTP timeout or 5xx Exponential-backoff retry queue (1min, 5min, 15min, 1hr); after 4 failures, notify with a copyable action-item list and a manual retry button. Never silently drop a push the user was told would happen
    Audio storage near capacity mid-meeting Storage watermark alarm at 80% Delete already-completed meetings' audio immediately (it was scheduled for deletion anyway) rather than pause capture on in-progress meetings; if still insufficient, disable real-time capture for new meeting starts and fall back to the conferencing platform's own native recording for post-hoc processing

    Tradeoff Analysis¶

    flowchart TD
    Q1{Does the org need\nlive transcript during\nthe meeting itself?} -->|Yes| TWOPHASE[Two-phase pipeline required:\nstreaming + offline]
    Q1 -->|No, async-only is fine| ONEPHASE[Post-meeting-only:\nsimpler, cheaper to run]

    Q2{Does the conferencing\nplatform expose\nper-participant streams?} -->|Yes, enterprise tier| PERPART[Per-participant streams:\ndiarization near-free]
    Q2 -->|No, consumer tier| MIXED[Mixed-audio diarization:\n20-30% higher DER]

    Q3{Is meeting volume\nhomogeneous enough to\njustify fine-tuning?} -->|Yes, high-volume,\nsingle domain| FINETUNE[Fine-tuned IE model:\nhigher F1, lower cost, narrow]
    Q3 -->|No, diverse\nmeeting types| LLMEX[General LLM extraction:\ngeneralizes across domains]
    1. Two-phase pipeline (real-time + post-meeting) vs. post-meeting-only. A post-meeting-only design processes audio once with the best available models, producing the highest achievable notes quality — but delivers zero during-meeting value. A participant who wants to know "what did Alice say about the timeline 10 minutes ago" has no way to find out mid-meeting. The two-phase architecture accepts running ASR and diarization twice specifically to serve two genuinely different needs at two genuinely different quality bars: the streaming pass produces the live view that's useful because it's immediate, and the offline pass produces the canonical record that's useful because it's accurate. The post-meeting notes merge the two — offline transcript replaces streaming transcript, with corrections tracked in the audit log — so the double-run cost buys both properties rather than forcing a choice between them.
    2. Per-participant audio streams vs. mixed audio for diarization. Per-participant streams (enterprise conferencing tiers) make diarization close to free — each stream is a speaker, no error-prone model in between. Mixed audio needs a diarization model to separate speakers and inherits its error rate. The tradeoff on the per-participant side: enterprise API access (a pricing premium), dependency on the conferencing vendor's API stability, and 4× the bandwidth. Mixed audio works on any WebRTC platform with no special access, at 20–30% higher DER. Default: per-participant streams where available (enterprise accounts), mixed-audio diarization as fallback (consumer accounts) — a two-tier quality experience that mirrors the underlying enterprise/consumer pricing split rather than fighting it.
    3. General LLM extraction vs. a fine-tuned information-extraction model for action items. A general LLM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) with structured-output prompts hits roughly 80% action-item F1 across diverse meeting types with zero per-domain training. A fine-tuned smaller model (BERT + CRF sequence labeler, trained on 50K annotated transcripts per domain) reaches 85–90% F1 on its specific domain at 20× lower inference cost — but needs expensive labeled data, degrades outside its training domain (an engineering-meeting-tuned model underperforms on sales calls), and carries ongoing retraining overhead. For a multi-domain platform serving heterogeneous enterprise clients, the LLM's cross-domain generalization at a modest quality discount is the right default; fine-tuned models become a premium offering for high-volume clients whose meeting types are homogeneous enough to justify the investment.
    4. Pre-computed embeddings vs. on-demand re-embedding for meeting search. Pre-computing and storing transcript-segment embeddings for every meeting (162GB/day, ~59TB/year at full retention, per Scale Estimation) makes search instantaneous for any meeting, at a storage cost that's the single largest line item in the system's storage footprint. On-demand embedding — computing embeddings only when a user actually searches into a given time range — trades that storage cost for query-time latency (embedding a meeting's transcript on the fly adds seconds to a search that pre-computed embeddings answer in milliseconds) and for repeated compute if the same meeting is searched multiple times. The platform's answer is neither extreme: pre-compute at ingestion for all meetings (search has to work day-one, and most search targets are recent), then apply a 90-day retention window on the embeddings specifically — not on the transcript, which stays for a year by default — so storage cost tracks actual search demand (concentrated in recent meetings) rather than total meeting volume. A user who wants to search a 6-month-old meeting can trigger on-demand re-embedding for that one meeting at query time, paying the latency cost only for the rare case that needs it.
    5. Confidence-gated action-item surfacing vs. a single hard threshold. Showing only high-confidence action items keeps the list trustworthy but under-delivers on recall — the 85% target in Requirements is unreachable if the platform silently drops every medium-confidence extraction. Showing everything above a low bar maximizes recall but risks training users to distrust the list, the same precision-erodes-trust dynamic the AI Customer Support Platform faces with its confidence-gated escalation threshold. The platform's answer is the same shape as that one: don't pick a single threshold, expose a visible confidence tier (high/medium) so the user decides how much to trust a borderline item, rather than the platform silently deciding on their behalf — the dial belongs to the person who has to act on the output, not to whoever tuned the extraction prompt.

    Interview Discussion¶

    This case study is asked specifically because it's easy to mistake for "the voice agent, but for meetings" — and the candidates who make that mistake describe a single real-time pipeline and stop. A weak answer treats transcription as the deliverable, describes one ASR pass, one diarization pass, and a summarization step bolted on afterward, and never explains why the system needs to process the same audio twice.

    A strong answer opens with the extraction-is-the-product thesis before describing any pipeline component — transcription is a means, action items and decisions are the end — and immediately identifies the two-path split (real-time for during-meeting availability, offline for archival accuracy) as the structural consequence of that thesis, not an arbitrary design choice. A strong answer also reasons concretely about why diarization has to run twice: the streaming pass's 20–30% DER is fine for a live "who's talking" indicator but not fine enough to reliably assign "I'll have that by Friday" to the right owner, and getting that assignment wrong is worse than getting it slow.

    Mid-level probes. "The real-time transcript shows 'Speaker 3' for the first ten minutes of a meeting — how does that become 'Alice Chen' by the time notes arrive?" Tests whether the candidate can walk the four-method speaker-resolution priority order — per-participant stream identity, voice enrollment, calendar-plus-self-introduction NLP, manual correction — rather than hand-waving "the model figures it out." "Someone says 'maybe someone should look into the API timeout issue' — is that an action item?" Tests whether the candidate can articulate the commitment-vs-suggestion distinction (first-person commitment or explicit assignment vs. a passive suggestion) and knows the right answer is to surface it at low confidence rather than silently drop it or silently promote it.

    Senior probes. "Action item recall dropped from 87% to 79% after last week's LLM provider model update — how do you find out why before users notice?" Tests whether the candidate reaches for the held-out annotated-transcript eval set and automatic-rollback gate (Model Layer) rather than waiting for user complaints to surface the regression — and whether they think to check if it's a structured-output formatting change (the model started returning slightly different JSON the parser silently mishandles) rather than a genuine capability regression. "A customer wants meetings translated live into Spanish for one Spanish-speaking participant — what changes?" Tests whether the candidate recognizes this is explicitly out of scope for the current architecture (Requirements) and can reason about where a translation layer would actually sit — after ASR, before the transcript assembler, as a parallel enrichment step — without conflating it with the existing English-only extraction pipeline, which would need its own multilingual handling for topic segmentation and action-item extraction, not just a translated transcript.

    Staff probes. "Your ASR bill is 99% of serving cost and revenue doesn't cover it — walk me through the actual levers, not just 'negotiate a better rate.'" Tests whether the candidate can reconstruct the Cost Model's logic unprompted: self-hosting the streaming leg (the offline leg is already self-hosted), the build-vs-buy threshold that decision rests on, and that pricing/packaging changes are a legitimate lever alongside infrastructure ones — not purely an engineering problem to route around. "A large customer says all recordings must be deleted within 24 hours, but they still want searchable meeting history six months later — is that possible?" Tests whether the candidate separates what's actually retained (structured notes and transcript, not audio — audio already deletes within ~2 hours by default) from what search depends on (transcript-segment embeddings, not audio), and can propose the ephemeral-audio-plus-retained-notes configuration from Security Layer as a direct answer rather than treating the two retention requirements as in conflict. A candidate who volunteers, unprompted, that this is really a question about which artifact carries the sensitivity — audio, transcript, or embeddings — rather than a single "how long do we keep data" policy, is operating at the depth this case study is built to surface.

    AI Recruiter — System Design Case Study¶

    Consider two recruiting platforms, both scoring the same applicant pool for the same open role.

    Platform A trains a resume-ranking model on historical hiring data: resumes of people who were hired, rated positively by the model as "good fits." It achieves high quality on the metric the team measures — recruiters report that top-ranked candidates are better fits than random selection. The model is never explicitly told about race, gender, or age.

    Six months later, an EEOC investigation begins. The historical hiring data reflected past discriminatory patterns. Candidates with names associated with certain ethnic backgrounds are statistically ranked lower. Candidates from HBCUs are penalized relative to candidates from PWIs with equivalent programs. Women with employment gaps — disproportionately caused by caregiving — are ranked lower. None of these signals were explicitly used as features. All of them were learned as proxies from the training data. The disparate impact is real. The liability is real.

    Platform B never builds a neural ranking model trained on historical hiring data. It builds an interpretable scoring pipeline instead: explicit criteria extracted from the job description, explicit weights set by the hiring team, explicit per-criterion scores derived from candidate qualifications — with protected-characteristic signals and their known proxies stripped before scoring ever runs. It is auditable, explainable, and continuously monitored for statistical disparate impact. It may produce slightly lower ranking quality on average than Platform A's neural model. It is the only design a legal team will approve.

    In AI recruiting, the model quality ceiling is set by what the legal department will approve, not by what the ML team can build. The blind scoring pipeline, the interpretable-model constraint, the mandatory human gate at every rejection, the continuous disparate-impact monitoring — every one of them follows from that ordering. This case study builds Platform B, and every component derives from the same ordering Platform A skipped: legal constraint first, ranking quality second. It is the direct structural analog of AI Healthcare Assistant, where HIPAA and the information/diagnosis boundary play the role anti-discrimination law and the disparate-impact doctrine play here.

    Requirements¶

    Functional — a four-stage hiring funnel, each stage with a different automation level:

    • Sourcing — proactively identify candidates from job boards, LinkedIn, GitHub, and the internal ATS for open roles, ranked against structured job requirements.
    • Screening — parse job descriptions into structured requirements, parse resumes into masked candidate profiles, score every inbound application against explicit weighted criteria, and present a ranked queue to recruiters. No rejection is sent without human approval.
    • Scheduling — coordinate interview availability between candidates and interviewers across calendars, time zones, and rescheduling, with minimal human involvement.
    • Evaluation support — summarize structured interviewer feedback into a coherent candidate summary for the hiring committee; make no hire/no-hire recommendation.

    Plus: JD parsing into structured, hiring-team-approved requirements; structured candidate scoring with a stored, criterion-by-criterion explanation for every decision; continuous disparate-impact monitoring across the funnel; integration with major ATS systems (Greenhouse, Lever, Workday).

    Non-functional

    Requirement Target Consequence of violation
    Autonomous rejections 0% — every rejection communication requires a human-approval token before it fires An AI-issued rejection with no human review is the precise fact pattern NYC Local Law 144 and Colorado's AI Act were written to prohibit
    Disparate impact monitoring 4/5 rule checked after every 100 decisions at each funnel stage A statistical disparity that goes undetected for a full hiring cycle is discovery material in a later EEOC charge, not a near-miss
    Explainability 100% of rejections have a stored, criterion-by-criterion reason before the notification sends An unexplainable rejection fails GDPR Article 22 and EEOC adverse-action documentation simultaneously
    Scheduling latency Interview booked within 24 hours of candidate availability confirmation A slow scheduling loop is the most common candidate-experience complaint in enterprise ATS deployments and directly extends time-to-fill
    Screening throughput Score and rank a full day's inbound volume (~3,300 applications) within the overnight batch window Screening is a batch pipeline, not a real-time query system — see Capacity Planning
    Masking integrity 0 protected-characteristic proxies (name, school-name-as-prestige, zip code, cover-letter text) reach the scoring pipeline A masking failure is functionally identical to Platform A above — it just takes longer to surface

    Explicitly out of scope: the final hiring decision, which is always made by humans; background-check processing (a separate regulated domain with its own FCRA obligations); compensation benchmarking (a separate product).

    Capacity Planning¶

    Modeled as a mid-to-large enterprise running continuous hiring across many teams simultaneously.

    Step Assumption Result
    Open roles 500 concurrent 500 roles
    Applications per role per month 200 100K applications/month
    Applications per day 100K / 30 ~3,300 applications/day
    Screening compute per application ~5 minutes (JD-match parsing + resume parsing + weighted scoring + explanation write) ~275 compute-hours/day
    Active scheduling threads 500 roles × 10 candidates in phone-screen or onsite stage at any time ~5,000 concurrent scheduling threads
    Disparate impact monitoring volume 100K decisions/month aggregated nightly ~3,300 records/night, batch job

    The screening pipeline is a batch job, not a real-time query system — 3,300 applications/day at 5 minutes of compute each fits comfortably in an overnight window with room to spare. This is the same shape of observation as AI Healthcare Assistant's capacity section: the number that actually strains the system is not throughput. It's the compliance infrastructure sitting on the critical path of every decision — the masking layer, the explanation write, the human-approval gate — none of which shrinks no matter how much the QPS number does.

    Scale Estimation¶

    • ATS candidate database. 100K applications/month × 50KB/application (structured masked profile + raw resume text, retained for audit purposes) = 5GB/month, growing to tens of GB/year.
    • Audit trail. 100K records/month × 5KB/record (criteria applied, weights, scores, recruiter approval, notification timestamp) = 500MB/month. EEOC record-keeping obligations require multi-year retention (commonly aligned to a jurisdiction's statute of limitations, typically several years); at a 5-year retention window that's ~30GB total — trivial storage, non-trivial legal obligation.
    • Demographic monitoring database. 100K records/month × 200 bytes (candidate ID + voluntarily self-reported demographics + stage outcome) = ~20MB/month. The size is trivial. What matters architecturally is that this store is physically separate from the screening database, with no code path connecting the two — the separation, not the byte count, is the design decision worth stating out loud in an interview.
    • JD structure store. 500 open roles × ~5KB structured requirement JSON = 2.5MB, negligible, but every version is retained — a JD's criteria and weights change over a role's life, and each version must be independently auditable against the rejections it produced.

    The automation level drops sharply between Stage 1 and Stage 2, and rises again between Stage 2 and Stage 3. A design that automates screening as aggressively as scheduling has built Platform A from the opening scenario.

    Detailed Design¶

    1. Anti-discrimination law as a hard architectural constraint¶

    US employment law (Title VII, ADEA, ADA) and its global analogs (GDPR, the UK Equality Act, the EU AI Act) prohibit discrimination on protected characteristics. The concept that actually drives the architecture is disparate impact (Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 1971): a facially neutral practice that has a disproportionate adverse effect on a protected group is unlawful unless the employer demonstrates business necessity and no less-discriminatory alternative exists. Critically, this doctrine does not require intent. A model does not need to use race as a feature to discriminate — it discriminates through proxies, learned from data or embedded in feature engineering, that correlate with protected characteristics without ever naming them.

    Proxy feature Protected characteristic it correlates with
    Candidate name (first name) Race, gender, national origin
    School attended (HBCU vs. PWI, state vs. Ivy) Race, socioeconomic background
    Zip code or neighborhood Race (redlining legacy)
    Employment gaps Gender (caregiving), disability
    Years since graduation Age
    Cover-letter vocabulary and grammar National origin, socioeconomic background
    Extracurriculars or sports listed Socioeconomic background, race

    Every one of these correlations is documented in hiring-bias research. A model trained to "predict good hires" on historical outcomes learns these proxies because they were present in the patterns it was trained on — the model isn't malfunctioning, it's doing exactly what it was optimized to do. The outcome is discriminatory regardless of intent. Four architectural consequences follow, and none of them is negotiable:

    1. The scoring pipeline never sees protected-characteristic signals or their known proxies. Candidate name is masked before the resume enters the scoring pipeline. School name is replaced with a school tier defined by objective, published rankings — never a legacy-correlated prestige proxy. Zip codes are dropped entirely. Employment gaps are preserved only as a duration ("18-month gap"), never with framing that implies cause. Cover-letter text is not scored. This masking is enforced at the data layer, in the parser that produces the candidate profile — not by an instruction telling the LLM to "ignore demographic information." A model can be prompted around an instruction; it cannot score a field that was never populated.

    2. Scores are attributable to explicit, job-related criteria. The scoring pipeline is not a black-box neural model. It's an interpretable scoring function: explicit criteria extracted from the JD, explicit weights set and documented by the hiring team, explicit per-criterion scores derived from candidate qualifications, summed into a total. Every rejection can be stated as "candidate did not advance because they scored below threshold on [specific criteria]" — satisfying both EEOC adverse-action documentation and GDPR Article 22's right to an explanation of automated decisions.

    3. No candidate is rejected without a human approval action. A recruiter must review and approve every batch of rejections before any rejection communication sends. The system cannot autonomously send a rejection — this is not a UX choice, it's a legal requirement in an increasing number of jurisdictions (NYC Local Law 144, Colorado's AI Act, and similar statutes elsewhere require human oversight of automated employment decision tools). The gate is architectural: the rejection-notification endpoint requires a human_reviewer_id and human_approval_timestamp before it will fire, and there is no code path that bypasses that check.

    4. Disparate impact is monitored continuously, not audited once. The system continuously computes pass-through rates by demographic group at every funnel stage, using demographic data that candidates voluntarily self-report and that is kept in a database strictly separate from the scoring pipeline. The EEOC's 4/5 rule is the trigger: if any protected group's pass-through rate falls below 80% of the highest-pass-through group's rate at any stage, a disparate-impact flag fires an automatic compliance investigation. The monitoring service reads only aggregated statistics — it never sees individual decisions attributed to a specific candidate's demographics.

    This is Chapter 18's analog to the healthcare chapter's "scope boundary enforced architecturally, not by prompting" — the argument the rest of this chapter is built to support.

    2. Resume-to-job matching as structured extraction, not semantic search¶

    The naive approach treats matching as a RAG problem — embed the JD, embed each resume, rank by cosine similarity. It fails for two reasons. First, semantic similarity doesn't capture structured requirements: a resume semantically similar to a software-engineering JD but missing the specific Python/AWS requirements will rank highly despite being a poor match on what the role actually needs. Second, a neural embedding model encodes exactly the proxy biases described in Concept 1 — names, schools, and writing style shift the embedding whether or not anyone intended them to.

    The correct architecture treats matching as structured extraction followed by explicit scoring, in four steps.

    Step 1 — JD parsing. An LLM-based extractor parses the natural-language job description into structured requirements:

    {
  "role": "Senior Backend Engineer",
  "required_skills": [
    {"skill": "Python", "min_years": 5, "hardness": "required"},
    {"skill": "AWS", "credential": "AWS Solutions Architect", "hardness": "required"}
  ],
  "preferred_skills": [
    {"skill": "Kubernetes", "min_years": 2, "hardness": "preferred"},
    {"skill": "Go", "hardness": "preferred"}
  ],
  "experience": {"min_years": 6, "type": "software engineering"},
  "education": {"min_level": "bachelor", "field": "CS or related", "hardness": "preferred"},
  "location": {"requirement": "US timezone", "remote": true}
}

    Ambiguity in the source JD must be resolved explicitly and documented — "5-7 years experience" becomes a required field where the hiring team states whether 5 is the hard floor or 7 is. The parsed structure is shown to the hiring team for approval before the pipeline runs against any candidate. This step also catches JD requirements that aren't actually job-related and therefore aren't legally defensible as screening criteria — a "must have graduated from a top-20 program" requirement gets flagged for hiring-team justification before it becomes a scoring weight.

    Step 2 — Resume parsing. Resume text is parsed into a candidate profile using the same schema as the JD requirements:

    {
  "candidate_id": "cand_xyz",
  "skills": [
    {"skill": "Python", "years_experience": 7, "context": "primary language at Roles 1 and 2"},
    {"skill": "AWS", "years_experience": 4, "certifications": ["AWS Solutions Architect Associate"]}
  ],
  "total_experience_years": 9,
  "education": {"level": "bachelor", "field": "Computer Science"}
}

    What's absent from this profile is the point: candidate name (masked), school name (replaced with tier), zip code (stripped), employment-gap framing (retained as duration only), cover-letter content (not scored). Masking happens at parse time — the downstream scoring pipeline receives only the masked profile and has no access to the raw resume.

    Step 3 — Explicit weighted scoring. The scoring function is a weighted sum over criteria, weights set per role by the hiring team:

    score = (weight_python × python_score)
      + (weight_aws × aws_score)
      + (weight_experience × experience_score)
      + ...

    Each component score is deterministic and auditable: 7 years of Python experience scores 1.0 on that component, 3 years scores 0.6, 0 years scores 0. Weights are documented per role and reviewed by legal/compliance before the pipeline runs against that role's applicants.

    Step 4 — Hard filter, then soft rank. Required criteria filter first — binary, meets or does not meet. Preferred criteria rank the surviving candidates. The output is a prioritized queue for human review, never a binary pass/fail decision.

    This architecture explicitly trades ranking quality for auditability. A neural model might rank marginally better on average; it cannot say why a specific candidate scored where they did, and "we don't fully know why the model ranked this way" is not a sentence a legal team can put in front of a regulator. Given the legal environment described in Concept 1, that tradeoff isn't close.

    3. The four-stage funnel, four different automation levels¶

    Getting the automation level wrong per stage — automating too much at Screening, too little at Sourcing or Scheduling — is the most common architectural mistake in AI recruiter deployments.

    Stage 1 — Sourcing (high automation appropriate). The AI proactively searches job boards, LinkedIn, GitHub, and the internal ATS for candidates matching the structured JD requirements, ranks them, and surfaces the list to recruiters. No rejection decisions happen here — a candidate who isn't sourced is simply never contacted, which carries far lower individual stakes than an active rejection. The anti-discrimination constraint still applies: search cannot use protected-characteristic signals as filters, and a GitHub profile (which can reveal name and therefore gender or race signals) is masked before its sourcing score is computed. Tools: the LinkedIn Recruiter API for external sourcing with skill and experience filters (never demographic filters), the internal ATS for the existing pipeline, GitHub/Stack Overflow for technical roles.

    Stage 2 — Screening (semi-automated, mandatory human gate). The highest-stakes stage. The AI scores every inbound application with the Concept 2 pipeline. Recruiters review the ranked list — sorting, filtering, inspecting per-criterion score breakdowns — and make every advance/reject decision themselves; the AI never decides autonomously. Advancing a batch requires the recruiter's auth token via "Advance selected"; rejecting a batch requires the same token via "Reject unselected," which triggers a review of the generated rejection reasons before anything sends. This stage handles the highest volume (thousands of applications per role at large companies) and carries the most legal exposure — the AI's job here is to rank and structure the queue, not to decide.

    Stage 3 — Scheduling (high automation appropriate). Pure calendar coordination, no discrimination risk, high automation appropriate. The scheduling agent reads interviewer calendars, proposes slots to candidates, handles rescheduling, sends reminders, and creates calendar invites: find_available_slots(interviewer_ids, duration, window) → send_proposal(candidate_id, slots) → book_slot(slot_id, all_participants). Integration surface: Google Calendar API, Outlook Graph API, Calendly-class services, and the ATS's own calendar hooks. Global candidate pools require careful timezone handling; reschedule requests are capped at a configurable count (typically 2-3) before the thread flags to the recruiter; no-shows trigger an automatic, polite re-engagement message rather than silently closing the thread.

    Stage 4 — Interview evaluation (AI-assisted, human-decided). The AI's role is structured note-taking and summarization, not evaluation. Interviewers fill in structured feedback forms after each interview; the AI summarizes structured feedback across multiple interviewers into one coherent candidate summary. The hiring decision is made by the hiring committee with no AI recommendation. An AI that recommends "advance" or "reject" at this stage, trained on how interviewers described different candidates, reintroduces exactly the learned-proxy problem Concept 1 exists to prevent — interviewer language about candidates is itself a channel bias can travel through. What the AI can do: summarize notes into a structured document, flag when interviewers gave significantly divergent ratings (a signal worth investigating, possibly for bias in one direction), and track time-to-decision. What it cannot do: synthesize feedback into a hire/no-hire call.

    4. Explainability as a legal requirement for rejection decisions¶

    A rejected candidate has the right to know why — under GDPR Article 22, NYC Local Law 144, and general adverse-action principles in anti-discrimination law. "The AI gave you a low score" is not a sufficient explanation. The architecture must produce a specific, criterion-by-criterion rejection reason for every rejected candidate, and it must do so as a direct read-out of the structured scoring record — not as something an LLM improvises at rejection time:

    Rejection reason for Candidate ID: cand_xyz
Role: Senior Backend Engineer

Required criteria:
- Python (5+ years required): 3 years found — below threshold [DID NOT MEET]
- AWS Solutions Architect certification: not found on application [DID NOT MEET]

Preferred criteria not evaluated because required criteria were not met.

This determination was reviewed and approved by: [Recruiter Name] on [Date].

    The explanation must be: in terms of job-related criteria only (never presentation, writing style, or "culture fit"); based on what was stated on the application, not inferred (if Python experience wasn't stated, the system notes "not stated on application," it does not assume zero); and consistent — two candidates with identical qualifications who were both rejected get the same reason.

    This constraint has three operational consequences: adverse-action notification requirements differ by jurisdiction (FCRA, EEOC, and NYC LL144 each specify different notice timing and format), so the architecture supports configurable notice templates per jurisdiction rather than one hardcoded template; the audit trail stores criteria applied, scores, weights, recruiter approval, notification sent, and timestamp for every rejection; and candidates have a right to request a more detailed explanation, served by a "request explanation" endpoint that returns the full structured scoring record.

    The explainability constraint is also what determines the model choice in Concept 2: a neural embedding model whose scores can't be attributed to specific criteria cannot legally be used for screening decisions. An interpretable scoring model with explicit criteria and weights can. This is the architectural fork that separates a legally defensible system from one that manufactures liability.

    5. Continuous bias audit and disparate impact monitoring¶

    A one-time bias audit at deployment is not sufficient — decisions aggregate over time into statistical patterns that reveal disparate impact even when each individual decision looked defensible in isolation. Monitoring has to be continuous and automatic.

    Data separation. Demographic monitoring data — voluntarily self-reported race/ethnicity, gender, disability status — lives in a database separate from the screening pipeline, with strict access controls: visible only to compliance officers for aggregate analysis, never to the scoring pipeline, never to recruiters reviewing individual candidates.

    Stage-level monitoring. After sufficient volume accumulates at each stage (minimum n=30 per group for statistical validity):

    pass_through_rate(group, stage) = candidates_advanced / candidates_evaluated

disparate_impact_ratio(group, stage) = pass_through_rate(group) / max(pass_through_rate across all groups)

EEOC 4/5 rule: flag if disparate_impact_ratio(group, stage) < 0.80

    A flag doesn't mean the system is discriminating — it means the pattern requires investigation. The compliance officer checks whether the disparity reflects a legitimate, job-related qualification gap between groups, or a criterion that turns out to be a proxy. The investigation outcome is documented, and criteria weights may be adjusted.

    Proactive synthetic testing. Monthly, the scoring pipeline runs on pairs of synthetic test resumes — identical in every job-relevant qualification (same Python years, same certifications, same experience level), differing only in a protected-characteristic signal: one name coded male, one female; one name coded white, one Black; one HBCU, one PWI with an equivalent program. Any statistically significant score difference between pair members is a bias-detection event — it means the masking pipeline failed to strip a proxy. This is more sensitive than aggregate funnel monitoring because it tests specific proxies directly rather than waiting for a pattern to emerge from real applicant volume.

    What happens when a flag fires: the compliance officer gets an automatic alert with the statistical summary; the affected stage's criteria and weights are frozen — the pipeline keeps running, but no criteria changes are made — pending investigation; the compliance officer reviews which criteria disproportionately filter the flagged group; each differential criterion is evaluated against the EEOC business-necessity standard, and if a less-discriminatory alternative exists, it replaces the criterion; the investigation outcome is documented regardless of result, because that documentation is the legal defense if the disparity is later challenged.

    API Design¶

    1. Screening batch decision — the human gate, as an API contract:

    POST /v1/screening/advance
Authorization: Bearer {recruiter_jwt}
{
  "recruiter_id": "rec_4471",
  "batch_id": "batch_role500_2026w29",
  "approved_candidates": ["cand_101", "cand_104"],
  "rejected_candidates": [
    {"candidate_id": "cand_102", "rejection_reason_code": "req_python_below_threshold"},
    {"candidate_id": "cand_103", "rejection_reason_code": "req_aws_cert_missing"}
  ]
}

Response:
{
  "batch_id": "batch_role500_2026w29",
  "approved_count": 2,
  "rejected_count": 2,
  "human_approval_timestamp": "2026-07-16T14:02:11Z",
  "notification_status": "queued"
}

    recruiter_id and human_approval_timestamp are not optional fields — the rejection-notification service refuses to fire without both present, and every rejected candidate already has a stored explanation (Concept 4) before this endpoint is ever called.

    2. Candidate explanation retrieval — the explainability guarantee, exposed:

    GET /v1/candidates/{id}/explanation

    This endpoint is role-restricted to compliance officers and returns only aggregate statistics — never an individual candidate's demographic data linked to their outcome. The same discipline shows up in AI Healthcare Assistant's audit endpoint returning only hashed patient identifiers: an endpoint built to monitor for a harm should not itself become a vector for a different one.

    Data Flow¶

    Path 1 — Application processed and rejected through the human gate:

    sequenceDiagram
    participant App as Applicant
    participant JDP as JD Parser
    participant RP as Resume Parser
    participant WS as Weighted Scorer
    participant EXP as Explanation Store
    participant REC as Recruiter
    participant RNS as Rejection Notification Service
    participant AUD as Audit Logger

    App->>RP: application submitted for role_500
    RP->>JDP: fetch approved structured JD for role_500
    JDP-->>RP: required and preferred criteria, weights_version v3
    RP->>RP: parse resume, mask name, school tier, zip
    RP->>WS: masked candidate profile
    WS->>WS: hard filter then weighted soft score
    WS->>EXP: write criterion-by-criterion scoring record
    EXP-->>WS: explanation stored
    WS->>REC: candidate added to ranked queue, score visible
    REC->>REC: reviews queue, selects reject with reason code
    REC->>RNS: POST advance batch, human_approval_timestamp set
    RNS->>EXP: confirm explanation already stored
    RNS->>App: rejection notice sent, jurisdiction-specific template
    RNS->>AUD: write full audit record

    Path 2 — Nightly disparate impact batch:

    sequenceDiagram
    participant AUD as Audit Trail: screening decisions
    participant DEMO as Demographic DB: separate store
    participant DIM as Disparate Impact Job
    participant COMP as Compliance Officer

    DIM->>AUD: read last 24h of screening decisions
    DIM->>DEMO: read voluntarily self-reported demographics, joined only in this batch context
    DIM->>DIM: compute pass_through_rate per group per stage
    DIM->>DIM: apply 4/5 rule
    alt ratio below 0.80
        DIM->>COMP: create compliance alert with statistical summary
        DIM->>AUD: freeze criteria weights for flagged stage
        COMP->>COMP: investigate, document outcome
    else ratio at or above 0.80
        DIM->>DIM: log clean result, no action
    end

    The two paths share nothing structurally — screening decisions never read the demographic database, and the nightly batch never writes back into the screening pipeline except to freeze weights pending investigation. That separation is deliberate: the code path that decides a candidate's fate and the code path that watches for bias in aggregate cannot be the same path, or the second stops being an independent check on the first.

    Retrieval Layer¶

    Two retrieval surfaces, both structured — neither is semantic search over free text.

    Surface 1 — Candidate sourcing search (external). Structured queries against the LinkedIn Recruiter API and job-board APIs: skill-based boolean filters ("Python" AND "AWS" AND "5+ years"), not semantic similarity against an embedded JD. This mirrors the drug-interaction-database pattern from AI Healthcare Assistant: some lookups are deterministic structured queries, not vector search, because the underlying relationship (does this candidate hold this credential) is boolean, not a matter of degree.

    Surface 2 — Internal ATS candidate search ("silver medalists"). When a new role opens, the ATS is searched for previously evaluated candidates who match the new role's requirements — strong candidates from past searches who weren't hired for a different role but fit this one. This is a structured filter query over the masked candidate database (skills, experience years, education tier), not a semantic search over resume text.

    Semantic search over raw resume text is deliberately avoided at both surfaces. An embedding model trained on general text encodes the same proxy biases — name, school, writing style — that the masking pipeline in Concept 1 exists to strip out. Compare with Enterprise RAG Platform, where semantic retrieval over a heterogeneous document corpus is the architectural core of the system: that pattern is correct there because the corpus carries no discrimination risk. It is the wrong pattern here, because the corpus is résumés and the risk is exactly what semantic embeddings are prone to encode.

    Agent Layer¶

    The scheduling agent is the primary agentic component — a multi-turn, tool-using agent managing interview-scheduling logistics end to end. It reads calendars (get_availability), proposes slots (send_availability_proposal), books confirmed slots (create_calendar_event), sends reminders (schedule_reminder), and handles rescheduling (reschedule_interview). This is a natural fit for the tool-use pattern described generally in Tool Use Architecture: each action is a discrete, auditable API call with a clear success/failure signal, and the agent's job is orchestrating a short sequence of them, not making a judgment call.

    The sourcing agent is a simpler loop: query source → score results against structured JD criteria → add to sourcing queue → repeat across sources. It runs autonomously because its output is a queue for recruiters to review, not a decision that affects anyone directly.

    No autonomous rejection agent exists anywhere in this system. Every advance/reject action requires a human token, as established in Concept 1. This is the sharpest agent-layer contrast with AI Customer Support Platform, where a confidence-gated agent autonomously resolves the majority of tickets and escalation is the exception path. Here the relationship inverts: the AI's autonomous path is structuring information (sourcing, scheduling, note summarization), and the consequential decision — reject this person — is the one path that is never automated, regardless of how confident the scoring model is.

    Model Layer¶

    Tier Role Notes
    1 — JD and resume parsers Convert natural-language JDs and resumes into structured JSON profiles A capable extraction model (GPT-4-class or Claude-class with structured output). Masking is enforced in the parser's output schema, not by a downstream filter — a field that was never extracted cannot leak.
    2 — Scoring function Compute the weighted, per-criterion candidate score Explicitly not a neural model. A linear weighted scoring function with interpretable, hiring-team-set weights. The scoring function is code, not ML — this is the load-bearing model choice in the entire chapter, forced by the explainability requirement in Concept 4.
    3 — Scheduling agent LLM Natural-language coordination with candidates over scheduling logistics A capable function-calling model (Claude Sonnet-class or GPT-4o-class). Low discrimination risk, high tolerance for a capable general-purpose model.

    The Tier 2 choice is worth stating explicitly in an interview: it is not that an interpretable model wasn't good enough to justify the engineering cost of a neural ranker, it's that a neural ranker is legally inadmissible for this decision regardless of its accuracy. See Fine-Tuning vs RAG for the general tradeoff space this chapter takes an unusually hard position within — most domains weigh quality against engineering cost; this one weighs quality against legal defensibility, and legal defensibility wins outright.

    Observability Layer¶

    Seven metrics, each carrying a distinct compliance or quality consequence:

    1. Funnel conversion rate by stage — sourcing → screening → phone screen → onsite → offer — the baseline health metric every recruiting org already tracks.
    2. Time-to-fill per role — the primary business metric this system is built to move.
    3. Disparate impact ratio by stage and protected group — the compliance metric, computed nightly per Concept 5, with any sub-0.80 ratio auto-escalated.
    4. Screening false-positive rate — candidates advanced by the AI's ranking but rejected at the next stage — signals screening criteria are too loose.
    5. Screening false-negative rate — estimated by tracking candidates who applied, were rejected, and were later hired by a competitor for a similar role. Hard to measure precisely, but directionally important: a persistent gap suggests the criteria are excluding strong candidates.
    6. Recruiter override rate — the fraction of AI-ranked top candidates the recruiter does not advance. A high rate signals the scoring criteria don't reflect what the recruiter actually values — see the Interview Discussion probe below for how this metric drives an investigation.
    7. Explanation completeness rate — the fraction of rejections with a complete, criterion-by-criterion reason stored before the notification fires. Anything below 100% is a release blocker, not a metric to trend over time.

    Security Layer¶

    Candidate PII is highly sensitive — name, contact information, work history, and often salary history — and is treated as PII under GDPR, CCPA, and comparable regimes. The demographic monitoring database carries additional sensitivity: self-reported race/ethnicity is among the most sensitive data categories under GDPR, which is precisely why its separation from the screening pipeline is enforced at the database permission level, not just by application logic — the screening pipeline has no credential that can reach the demographic database at all.

    • Recruiter UI shows candidate names and contact info (for outreach purposes, once a candidate has advanced past the masked-scoring stage) but never self-reported demographics.
    • Compliance officer UI shows aggregate demographic statistics but never individual candidate-level demographics linked to a specific application outcome.
    • Right to erasure. A candidate can request deletion of their application data. Erasure removes the raw application and PII; the anonymized aggregate contribution to the disparate-impact statistics is retained, because removing it would invalidate the aggregate statistic itself — GDPR's legitimate-interest basis applies to that retained aggregate, the same way de-identified analytics are handled in AI Security Architecture.
    • Masking integrity as a security property, not just a fairness property. A masking-pipeline bug that lets a name or school-name-as-prestige leak into the scoring pipeline is functionally a PII exposure inside a decision system, and is treated with the same severity as a credential leak — it triggers the same freeze-and-investigate path as a disparate-impact flag.

    Cost Model¶

    Component Monthly cost % of total
    JD + resume parsing (LLM extraction, ~100K applications/month) ~$1,000 < 1%
    Weighted scoring (deterministic code, compute only) ~$300 < 1%
    Scheduling agent LLM (~5,000 active threads, multi-turn) ~$900 < 1%
    Sourcing agent (external API + scoring, ~50K candidates/month) ~$400 < 1%
    Infra (ATS DB, audit trail storage, search infra) ~$1,800 < 1%
    Monthly synthetic bias testing (test-pair generation + scoring) ~$300 < 1%
    Recruiter screening review time (100K applications/month × ~2 min avg × fully-loaded labor rate) ~$166,000 83%
    Compliance officer, legal review of criteria/weights, EEOC monitoring ~$30,000 15%
    Total ~$200,700/month

    The pattern is the same inversion documented in AI Healthcare Assistant: recruiter review time and compliance overhead together are 98% of total cost, while every AI component combined — parsing, scoring, scheduling, sourcing, bias testing — is under 2%. The difference from the healthcare chapter is why: healthcare's human cost is driven by reviewing high-risk clinical outputs, a fraction of total volume. Recruiting's human cost is driven by the fact that 100% of rejections require human review, by legal mandate, not by a risk threshold. A budget that plans for AI inference cost and treats recruiter time as a rounding error has misread which line item this system actually spends money on.

    Failure Handling¶

    Failure mode Detection Response
    Explanation store unavailable at rejection time Write failure on EXP write path Block the rejection notification entirely — a rejection without a stored explanation is a compliance gap, not a degraded-mode UX tradeoff. Advance decisions are unaffected since they don't require an explanation.
    Masking pipeline leaks a proxy (detected via synthetic testing or audit) Monthly synthetic-pair score divergence, or manual audit finding Freeze the affected stage's scoring immediately, halt new rejections at that stage pending investigation, and re-run the affected time window's decisions through a corrected parser once fixed. Treated with the same severity as a credential leak (Security Layer).
    Disparate impact monitoring job fails to run Nightly job health check Screening continues under human review regardless — the human gate doesn't depend on monitoring. But compliance is alerted immediately, and if monitoring isn't restored within 24 hours, new rejection batches for the affected roles require compliance-officer sign-off until it is.
    JD parser produces an ambiguous or legally risky criterion (e.g., an unstated proxy for age or a "culture fit" field) Hiring-team review step before pipeline activation, or a flagged-criteria heuristic The pipeline does not run against any applicants for that role until the hiring team resolves the ambiguity or removes the flagged criterion.
    ATS integration API (Greenhouse/Lever/Workday) unavailable Integration health check, error rate > threshold Inbound applications queue rather than drop; screening resumes automatically once the integration recovers. No fallback scoring runs without confirmed, current job requirements from the ATS.
    Scheduling agent gets no candidate response after configured reschedule attempts Reschedule-attempt counter Escalate the thread to the recruiter rather than looping indefinitely; the agent never silently drops a candidate.
    Recruiter review queue backlog grows past a depth threshold Queue-depth monitoring Alert the hiring manager; the system never relaxes the human-gate requirement to clear a backlog faster — a growing queue is a staffing problem to solve, not a signal to loosen the gate.

    Tradeoff Analysis¶

    flowchart TD
    Q1{"Does this candidate signal correlate\nwith a protected characteristic?"} -->|No| SCORE["Include in scoring pipeline\nwith a documented weight"]
    Q1 -->|Yes| Q2{"Does a job-related, non-correlated\nproxy exist?"}
    Q2 -->|Yes| REPLACE["Replace with the job-related proxy\ne.g. school tier, not school name"]
    Q2 -->|No| EXCLUDE["Exclude entirely from scoring"]

    1. Neural ranking vs. interpretable scoring. Neural ranking produces higher matching quality but can't be explained at the criterion level and encodes proxy biases from training data. Interpretable scoring has a lower quality ceiling but is legally defensible. The legal environment makes this tradeoff explicit rather than a matter of taste — interpretable scoring is the only compliant choice for a system that makes rejection decisions.

    2. Hard filters vs. soft ranking for minimum requirements. Hard filters are legally defensible — a stated minimum is uniformly applied and easy to document — but disqualify candidates who are excellent on other dimensions. Soft ranking is more flexible but requires defending, case by case, why a below-minimum candidate wasn't advanced while another was. The pipeline uses hard filters for genuinely required criteria and soft ranking for everything else, precisely to keep that defense simple where it needs to be simple.

    3. Proactive sourcing vs. inbound-only. Proactive sourcing reaches more, and potentially better, candidates but introduces the risk that the sourcing criteria are themselves discriminatory. Inbound-only avoids that risk but shrinks the candidate pool. The correct answer is proactive sourcing with audited, structured criteria — not inbound-only — because the discrimination risk is manageable with the same masking and audit discipline applied to screening, while a shrunk pool is a permanent quality cost with no mitigation.

    4. Full automation of scheduling vs. human-assisted. Full automation is faster and scales, but a fully automated agent handling rescheduling, timezone confusion, and candidate questions needs robust failure-mode handling — a non-responsive candidate or a failed calendar integration can't be allowed to silently strand a thread. Human-assisted scheduling is slower but more resilient. Given scheduling's low discrimination stakes, the tradeoff resolves toward automation with a hard escalation backstop (Failure Handling), not toward human-assisted by default.

    5. Centralized vs. per-role bias monitoring. Monitoring at the role level catches role-specific issues but has low statistical power for any individual role (small n). Monitoring at the department or pipeline level has higher statistical power but can mask a role-specific problem inside a healthy aggregate. The system runs both: role-level monitoring where volume supports it (n≥30), rolling up to department-level monitoring as the higher-power backstop that catches what individual roles can't detect alone.

    Interview Discussion¶

    This case study tests something no other chapter in this section does directly: a system whose quality ceiling is set by legal constraints, not technical ones. A candidate who designs the highest-quality neural ranking system has built the wrong thing.

    A weak answer opens with: "I'd train a model on historical hire data and rank candidates by predicted hire likelihood." This is exactly the system that creates disparate-impact liability in the opening scenario — it's not a rough draft of the right answer, it's the wrong answer. A strong answer opens instead with: "Before any model design, I need to establish which features are legally permissible to use as scoring criteria — and explicitly audit for proxy variables that encode protected characteristics." It treats the masking pipeline, the human gate, and the interpretable-scoring constraint as the starting point of the design, not a compliance review applied to a design that's already been chosen.

    Mid-level probes to expect:

    "Why isn't 'we removed the candidate's name' sufficient for fairness?" Tests whether the candidate understands the proxy problem from Concept 1 — school name, zip code, and employment-gap framing all carry the same signal a name would, through a different channel, and a masking pipeline that stops at the name has done a small fraction of the necessary work.

    "Why not just embed resumes and job descriptions and rank by similarity — it's a much simpler pipeline?" Tests whether the candidate can articulate both failure modes from Concept 2: semantic similarity doesn't capture structured requirements (a resume can be topically similar but miss the actual required skill), and a general embedding model encodes exactly the proxy biases the masking pipeline exists to strip.

    Staff-level probes:

    "Your disparate impact monitoring flags that Black candidates are advancing from screening to phone screen at 68% the rate of white candidates. Walk me through your investigation." Tests recognition that 0.68 is a 4/5-rule violation (below the 0.80 threshold) and a concrete investigation sequence: check whether the applicant pool's qualification distribution actually differs by group for this role (and if so, why); run synthetic test pairs — identical qualifications, differing name/school signals — to check whether the masking pipeline itself is failing; identify which specific criteria produce the largest score differential across groups; evaluate each differential criterion against the EEOC business-necessity standard and replace it with a less-discriminatory alternative if one exists. Document the investigation and its outcome regardless of what it finds.

    "A recruiter tells you the AI's top-ranked candidates are consistently not the ones they end up interviewing — they keep digging into the middle of the ranked list to find good candidates. What does this tell you, and what do you do?" Tests recognition that a high recruiter-override rate (Observability metric 6) signals the scoring criteria don't reflect what the recruiter actually values. The response: interview recruiters to surface the implicit criteria driving their choices; if those criteria are job-related and legally permissible, add them to the scoring model with explicit, documented weights; if they turn out to be protected-characteristic proxies — "culture fit" that in practice correlates with demographic similarity to the existing team — do not add them, and instead help the recruiter articulate the underlying, job-related competency they're actually trying to assess.

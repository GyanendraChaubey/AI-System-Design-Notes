    Web Crawler for AI Knowledge Ingestion — System Design Case Study¶

    Every other data-layer chapter in this section operates on content that already exists as clean text: RAG System opens with a 10M-document corpus and starts chunking it; Vector Database opens with a stream of already-embedded vectors and starts indexing them. Neither asks where the documents came from. This chapter is where they come from. It is the only chapter in this book that is purely about data acquisition — no model in the generation path, no serving system, no index of its own beyond the bookkeeping needed to run itself — and every other data-layer chapter in Section 25 is, whether it says so or not, a downstream consumer of what this one produces. AI Search Engine is the clearest example: that chapter's Retrieval Layer describes a freshness-tiered scheduler and a content-hash change detector as its own mechanism, then explicitly hands off "the mechanics of fetching, parsing, and extraction" to this chapter. Read the other direction, the relationship is: Chapter 15 specifies what a crawl system must produce for a live search index to work — freshness SLOs, a change signal, a Tier 0/1/2 priority scheme tuned to query-serving needs. This chapter is how you build the system that produces it, at a level of mechanism Chapter 15 deliberately doesn't re-derive, and generic enough to also feed RAG System's ingestion pipeline, Vector Database's write path, and Perplexity's live-fetch grounding — three consumers with different freshness needs and none of them the crawler itself.

    The insight that shapes every design decision below: a web crawler built for AI knowledge ingestion has fundamentally different quality requirements than a web crawler built for search. Googlebot can afford to be noisy, because a ranking model consumes its output at retrieval time and that model is trained to discount boilerplate, navigation chrome, and ad text when it scores a page against a query — the noise gets filtered downstream, at ranking time, by a system built to tolerate it. An AI knowledge ingestion crawler has no equivalent downstream filter. Boilerplate that lands in a RAG corpus becomes a retrieved chunk a user reads as an answer; boilerplate that lands in an LLM training set becomes a pattern the model learns to reproduce. There is no ranking stage standing between "the crawler kept this text" and "a model treated this text as ground truth." That single fact reshapes four decisions that a generic crawler design gets to treat as afterthoughts: URL prioritization has to favor content-dense pages over thin archives (Concept 1 below), content extraction has to precisely isolate the main-content region rather than harvest all visible text (Concept 2), deduplication has to be more aggressive than a search index strictly requires (Concept 3), and the output format has to be structured, semantically annotated Markdown rather than a flat text dump (also Concept 2) — because the chunker and embedding model reading this output have no ranking model standing between them and whatever the crawler decided to keep.

    The second thing that makes this chapter different from every other one in Section 25: politeness and legal compliance are hard technical constraints here, not deployment afterthoughts layered on at the end. robots.txt compliance, Crawl-delay enforcement, per-domain rate limiting, and terms-of-service awareness are load-bearing parts of the architecture, covered at the same depth as the distributed-systems mechanics — because getting them wrong doesn't degrade a metric, it gets the crawler's IP ranges blocked and exposes the organization operating it to real legal liability. Security Layer below treats this with more weight than the equivalent section in any other chapter in this book.

    Requirements¶

    Functional

    • Discover new URLs continuously from a seed list and by extracting outlinks from already-crawled pages, at a sustained rate the frontier can prioritize and schedule without unbounded queue growth.
    • Respect robots.txt Disallow directives and Crawl-delay for every domain, as a blocking gate on the frontier rather than a filter applied after the fact.
    • Deduplicate content at both the exact level (identical bytes) and the near-duplicate level (syndicated copies, minor rewrites, template-generated pages) before anything reaches the output stream.
    • Extract the main content of every fetched page as structured Markdown, with metadata (title, author, publish date, language, content type) attached, discarding navigation, ads, and boilerplate.
    • Publish clean document records to a durable stream consumed by downstream ingestion pipelines — this system does not embed, chunk, or index anything itself.
    • Schedule re-crawls per URL based on an estimated change rate, rather than a single fixed interval applied uniformly across the corpus.

    Non-functional

    • Crawl throughput: 10,000 pages/second sustained across the fleet.
    • Freshness: Tier 0 (push-notified) content delivered to the downstream consumer within 5 minutes of publication; Tier 1 (high-frequency) within 4 hours; Tier 2 (standard) within 7 days.
    • Deduplication coverage: greater than 99% of exact duplicates caught before publication; greater than 90% of near-duplicates (Hamming distance ≤ 3 under SimHash) caught.
    • Extraction quality: greater than 85% main-content recall, measured against a labeled evaluation set of pages with hand-annotated content regions.
    • Politeness: zero robots.txt violations; no domain's fetch rate ever exceeds its declared (or default) Crawl-delay.

    Explicitly out of scope: embedding and indexing extracted content — that begins where RAG System's ingestion pipeline picks up this system's output; ANN index management, covered in Vector Database; and any generation or synthesis over the crawled corpus, which is the concern of consumers like AI Search Engine and Perplexity, not of the acquisition layer itself.

    Capacity Planning¶

    Sized for a 100-billion-URL frontier at 10,000 pages/second sustained throughput, using the method from GPU Sizing & Capacity Planning:

    Step Assumption Result
    Tracked URL corpus Frontier size, all tiers combined 100B URLs
    Sustained crawl throughput Target fleet-wide fetch rate 10,000 pages/sec
    Average page size Raw HTML fetched per page ~50KB
    Average extracted size Post-extraction structured Markdown ~5KB
    Inbound bandwidth 10,000 pages/sec × 50KB ~500MB/s
    Crawl status store 100B URLs × ~200 bytes/entry (hash, timestamps, tier, change-rate estimate) ~20TB
    Seen-URL Bloom filter 100B entries at 0.1% false-positive rate ~1.4TB memory

    Fetcher fleet sizing. At an average fetch latency of 300ms (network round-trip plus server response time, before any extraction work begins), one fetcher connection completes roughly 1 / 0.3s ≈ 3 pages/second. Sustaining 10,000 pages/second therefore needs 10,000 / 3 ≈ 3,300 concurrent fetcher connections. At a practical density of 100 concurrent connections per machine (bounded by open-socket and memory overhead per connection, not CPU), that's 3,300 / 100 ≈ 33 fetcher machines.

    Extraction fleet sizing. Per-page extraction latency is a weighted average across the three tiers covered in Concept 2 below: Tier 1 (heuristic, ~75% of pages) at 50ms, Tier 2 (ML-based, ~20% of pages) at 150ms, Tier 3 (headless rendering, ~5% of pages) at 500ms. Weighted: 0.75 × 50 + 0.20 × 150 + 0.05 × 500 = 37.5 + 30 + 25 ≈ 92.5ms, rounding to ~100ms average. At 10,000 pages/second, that's 10,000 × 0.1s = 1,000 concurrent extraction operations. At 50 worker threads per machine, 1,000 / 50 = 20 extraction machines — separate from the small, dedicated headless-browser pool sized in Concept 2, which handles only the 5% of pages needing Tier 3.

    The fetcher and extraction fleets alone — 33 + 20 ≈ 53 machines — are a modest footprint relative to the 45,000-CPU-core ANN fleet AI Search Engine sizes for query-time retrieval over the corpus this crawler feeds. Acquisition is cheap; what gets built on top of it is not — a theme Cost Model returns to below.

    Scale Estimation¶

    • Bloom filter cluster: 1.4TB for the seen-URL check (from Capacity Planning), sharded across a Redis cluster via consistent hashing — sized and operated in Retrieval Layer below.
    • SimHash near-duplicate index: of the 100B tracked URLs, exact deduplication (Concept 3) typically removes a large share before near-duplicate detection ever runs — a realistic working figure is ~10B genuinely distinct extracted documents surviving to the SimHash stage. At 18 permutation tables × 10B entries × 8 bytes/fingerprint, that's ~1.4TB — coincidentally close to the Bloom filter's footprint, and colocated with it operationally for the same reason: both are hot, in-memory, high-QPS lookup structures with no durability requirement beyond periodic snapshotting.
    • Crawl status store: 20TB (from Capacity Planning), holding one row per tracked URL — the source of truth freshness scheduling reads from and writes back to on every crawl.
    • Kafka crawled-documents throughput: 10,000 documents/second × ~5KB extracted content each ≈ 50MB/second — comfortably served by a 3-broker cluster with room to spare; this is the handoff channel detailed in Concept 5.
    • Downstream embedding cost ceiling: 10,000 documents/second at ~5KB extracted text (~1,000 tokens/document) is 10M tokens/second flowing toward whatever ingestion pipeline embeds it. At $0.0001/1K tokens, that's \(1/second ≈ **\)86,400/day — a theoretical ceiling assuming every fetched page is embedded on every crawl. In practice this never happens: change detection (Concept 4) means only pages whose content-hash actually changed get re-embedded, and not every one of 100B tracked URLs is re-crawled daily in the first place. The real embedding bill scales with the change-rate-weighted re-crawl volume**, not the raw fetch rate — the same "confirm-unchanged is nearly free, re-embed only what moved" principle AI Search Engine applies at its own scale, mechanized here in more depth.

    High Level Design¶

    Three pipeline stages, each independently scalable, connected by durable state rather than direct calls — no stage blocks on another's latency, and a slowdown in extraction never backs up into the fetcher fleet beyond Kafka's buffering capacity.

    Reading the diagram as a funnel: of every 100 URLs discovered, a meaningful fraction never reach a fetcher at all — already-seen URLs are dropped at the Bloom filter, disallowed paths are dropped at the robots.txt gate — and of what does get fetched, exact and near-duplicate detection remove more before a document ever reaches Kafka. Each drop point is cheaper than the one before it in the pipeline (a Bloom filter lookup is microseconds; a fetch is hundreds of milliseconds; extraction is tens to hundreds of milliseconds more), which is why the gates are ordered cheapest-first: nothing expensive happens to a URL that a cheap check would have rejected anyway.

    Two stores feed back into Stage 1 rather than sitting passively downstream: the Crawl Status Store supplies the next_crawl_time that determines whether a previously-crawled URL re-enters the frontier, and the Bloom Filter Store is written to the moment a URL is canonicalized, before it's known whether the fetch will even succeed — this is deliberate, covered in Concept 1 below.

    Detailed Design¶

    The five subsections below are the load-bearing mechanisms of this system — each one is where the "AI ingestion crawler ≠ search crawler" argument from the introduction turns into an actual design decision, not just a stated principle.

    Concept 1 — Crawl frontier management¶

    At 100 billion tracked URLs, the frontier — the persistent, priority-ordered, politeness-aware queue of URLs waiting to be fetched — is the hardest distributed-systems problem in the entire system, and it's the part most interview answers reduce to "a queue" and get wrong.

    URL deduplication at scale. Before any URL is admitted to the frontier, the system checks whether it has already been seen. A naive in-memory hash set doesn't fit: 100B URLs × ~50 bytes each ≈ 5TB, too large to hold as an exact set on any single tier of infrastructure without real cost. The production answer is a Bloom filter (a Cuckoo filter if deletions are ever needed — they generally aren't here, since "seen" is monotonic). At 100B elements and a 0.1% false-positive rate, the filter needs roughly 1.4TB, spread across an in-memory Redis cluster — affordable, and O(1) per lookup. False positives are harmless by construction: occasionally treating an unseen URL as seen just means it gets rediscovered later from a different inlink and reconsidered then, rather than being permanently lost.

    Canonicalization runs before the dedup check, not after. https://example.com/page, http://example.com/page, https://example.com/page?utm_source=twitter, and https://www.example.com/page frequently resolve to the same document, and treating them as four distinct URLs multiplies both frontier size and crawl load for zero additional coverage. Canonicalization lowercases scheme and host, strips known tracking parameters (utm_*, fbclid, gclid, and similar), resolves protocol-relative URLs, and follows HTTP 301 redirect chains to their final destination before the Bloom filter is ever consulted. A crawler that skips this step re-fetches the same page under every UTM-tagged social-share variant that ever links to it — a real and common source of wasted crawl budget at scale, not a hypothetical edge case.

    URL priority scoring. Not every admitted URL deserves equal crawl priority. Priority is a weighted composite:

    \[P(\text{url}) = w_1 \cdot \text{domain\_authority} + w_2 \cdot \text{content\_quality} + w_3 \cdot \text{freshness\_urgency} + w_4 \cdot \text{discovery\_depth}\]
    • Domain authority (0–1): estimated from inlink count and the historical content quality of prior crawls of the domain. Wikipedia scores near 1.0; a domain discovered five minutes ago scores near 0.
    • Content quality signal (0–1): estimated content density inferred from the URL shape and prior crawls of the same domain. Documentation pages, technical blogs, and encyclopedic content score high; paginated archive pages (/page/1, /page/2, ...), thin product listings, and login-walled pages score low. This is the term that most directly encodes the "AI ingestion crawler favors content-dense pages" principle from the introduction — a generic web crawler has no equivalent reason to discount /page/47 of a forum thread archive, but this one does, because that page is far more likely to degrade a RAG corpus than to answer a query usefully.
    • Freshness urgency (0–1): time since last crawl, scaled by the URL's estimated change rate from Concept 4 below — a fast-changing page crawled six hours ago carries more urgency than a static reference page crawled yesterday.
    • Discovery depth: URLs discovered from a high-priority page inherit elevated priority. A link surfaced from a Wikipedia article is a stronger signal than a link surfaced from a comment section, even before that link's own domain authority is known.

    Weights are tuned to the crawl's mission — a news-oriented crawl weights freshness urgency heavily; a documentation-oriented crawl weights content quality heavily — and this is the one place in the frontier where mission-specific tuning belongs; everything else below is mechanism that doesn't change with mission.

    The front-queue / back-queue politeness architecture. The hard constraint is: no more than one request per second to any single domain, unless that domain's robots.txt specifies a different Crawl-delay. A single global priority queue cannot enforce this cleanly — nothing stops it from dequeuing a hundred consecutive URLs that all happen to belong to the same domain, since priority score has no domain-awareness built in. The standard fix decouples priority ordering from domain politeness into two tiers:

    TIMESTAMP --> TSCHECK2

    The front queue is a set of priority buckets (10 levels is a typical granularity) — each a persistent, disk-backed FIFO. A URL's priority score (above) determines which bucket it lands in. The back queues are one per active registered domain, each a bounded FIFO (capped around 1,000 URLs) holding that domain's pending work; when a back queue runs low, it refills from the highest-priority front-queue bucket that has entries for that domain.

    The scheduler loop that ties them together: (1) find a back queue whose domain's last_fetch_time has aged past its crawl_delay; (2) dequeue one URL from it and dispatch to a fetcher; (3) stamp last_fetch_time = now for that domain; (4) if the back queue is now empty, refill it from the front queue. This gives three guarantees simultaneously: no domain is ever fetched faster than its crawl delay (enforced structurally, not by a rate limiter bolted on after the fact), dispatch is O(1) per URL with no global scan, and priority ordering is preserved across the whole frontier despite being enforced per-domain at dispatch time.

    robots.txt compliance is a gate, not a filter. robots.txt is fetched and parsed for every new domain before any page on it is admitted to a back queue, and cached for 24 hours (it changes rarely enough that this is safe, and often enough that a longer TTL risks missing a legitimate policy change). Three directives matter: Disallow paths, which are excluded from the frontier entirely — never fetched and discarded after the fact, but never admitted in the first place; Crawl-delay, which overrides the default 1-second domain delay when present; and Sitemap, whose URLs seed the frontier with the domain's own signal about what it considers worth indexing (used again in Concept 4). A URL that hasn't cleared this gate cannot enter any back queue — the gate is structural, not a policy a fetcher worker could choose to skip.

    Concept 2 — Content extraction¶

    This is where an AI ingestion crawler diverges furthest from a general-purpose one. The goal is not "get the text off the page" — it's extract only the main content, preserve its semantic structure, and discard everything else, where "everything else" is typically 30–70% of a page's HTML text: navigation, headers, footers, sidebars, cookie banners, ad slots, share buttons, related-article carousels, pagination controls.

    Why this is hard. HTML markup does not reliably signal content regions. <div class="article-body"> is the main content on a news site and a sidebar widget on someone else's blog — CSS class names are a developer's naming choice, not a semantic contract, and any extractor that trusts markup structure over content signals will be wrong often enough to matter at corpus scale.

    Tier 1 — heuristic extraction (~75% of pages, ~50ms). Every text block in the DOM gets scored on a handful of content-agnostic features: character density (non-markup characters ÷ total characters in the block — article prose runs around 0.7, navigation menus around 0.1, since menus wrap few words in many tags); link density (characters inside <a> tags ÷ total block characters — navigation and sidebars run around 0.8, article bodies around 0.05); block size (very short blocks, under ~10 words, are usually labels or nav items, not content); and position in the DOM tree (content blocks cluster as siblings under a common parent; boilerplate clusters inside <header>, <nav>, <footer> containers). Boilerpipe and JusText implement this as a decision tree over text blocks and work well on news articles, technical docs, and encyclopedic pages — and fail on unusual layouts, e-commerce pages mixing prose with spec tables, and JavaScript-rendered single-page apps whose initial HTML has no content to score in the first place.

    Tier 2 — ML-based extraction (~20% of pages, ~100–200ms). Trafilatura is representative: a model trained on over a million labeled CommonCrawl pages that extracts both the content region and structured metadata (author, publish date, language, content type) in one pass, outperforming heuristic extraction on the layouts Tier 1 struggles with. Escalation trigger: Tier 1's own output is scored for plausibility — if the extracted block is suspiciously short (under ~100 words) or has unexpectedly high link density (over ~0.3), that's a signal Tier 1 picked the wrong region, and the page escalates to Tier 2 rather than shipping a low-confidence Tier 1 result.

    Tier 3 — headless browser rendering (~5% of pages, ~500ms). For pages where content only exists after JavaScript runs — single-page apps, infinite-scroll feeds, paywall-gated reveal patterns — Playwright or Puppeteer renders the full DOM in a real browser context, waits for a stable-DOM or network-idle signal, and hands the rendered HTML back to Tier 1 or Tier 2 for the actual extraction pass. Detecting the need for Tier 3 cheaply, before paying for a browser context: compare the text volume in the raw HTTP response against what a full render would produce — if that ratio is below roughly 0.3, the page is very likely JavaScript-rendered, and it's worth the cost of a headless context. This detection matters because a headless browser context costs roughly one CPU core and 200MB of RAM for its duration — expensive enough that a dedicated, separately-sized pool exists for exactly the 5% of pages that need it, rather than running every page through a browser "just in case," which the escalation logic above exists specifically to avoid.

    flowchart TB
    HTML["Raw HTML"] --> TIER1["Tier 1: Heuristic Extraction - Boilerpipe or JusText"]
    TIER1 --> CONF1{"Extraction Confidence Above Threshold?"}
    CONF1 -->|"Yes - about 75% of pages"| MARKDOWN["Structured Markdown Output"]
    CONF1 -->|"No - short text or high link density"| TIER2["Tier 2: ML Extraction - Trafilatura"]
    TIER2 --> CONF2{"Extraction Confidence Above Threshold?"}
    CONF2 -->|"Yes - about 20% of pages"| MARKDOWN
    CONF2 -->|"No - JavaScript-rendered page suspected"| TIER3["Tier 3: Headless Browser Rendering"]
    TIER3 --> REEXTRACT["Re-run Tier 1 or Tier 2 on Rendered DOM"]
    REEXTRACT --> MARKDOWN
    MARKDOWN --> SCHEMA["Output Schema: url, content_hash, body_markdown, metadata"]

    Structured Markdown, not a flat text dump. Whichever tier produces the final extraction, the output is converted to Markdown before it leaves this system: <h1>–<h6> become #–######, <ul>/<ol> become -/1. lists, <pre>/<code> becomes fenced code blocks, <table> becomes a Markdown table with a header separator row, <strong>/<b> becomes **bold**. This is not a formatting nicety — it's a contract with the downstream chunker. A chunker reading flat text has no signal for where a section boundary is, no way to tell that a block of text is code rather than prose (an embedding model will happily embed a code block as if it were a paragraph, degrading its representation of both), and no way to preserve which header a table cell's value belongs to. A chunker reading the Markdown this system emits can split on heading boundaries and treat fenced code blocks as a distinct unit — the same distinction Chunking Strategies treats as structure-aware chunking's precondition. Getting this contract right here is what lets every chunking strategy downstream — hierarchical parent-child chunking in RAG System, structure-aware splitting elsewhere — actually work as designed, rather than degrading to fixed-size splitting because the input has no exploitable structure left in it.

    Concept 3 — Near-duplicate detection¶

    Duplicates in an AI-facing corpus are more damaging than in a search index: a RAG system retrieves the same fact five times instead of five distinct facts, wasting context budget a generator could have spent on genuinely different evidence, and an LLM training corpus over-weights whatever got duplicated, disproportionately memorizing it. Detection runs as three stages of increasing cost and decreasing throughput, each one a backstop for what the stage before it misses.

    Stage 1 — exact deduplication, O(1) per document. SHA-256 over the extracted content (post-extraction, pre-chunking — the same hash that Concept 4 reuses for change detection, described there) is looked up against a distributed hash set with a TTL matched to the corpus's expected lifetime. A hit discards the document immediately. This catches mirror sites, identically-syndicated articles, paginated pages that render the same content under different query strings, and URL-alias pairs that survived canonicalization — a large fraction of raw duplicate volume, caught before any more expensive stage runs.

    Stage 2 — near-duplicate detection with SimHash, roughly O(log N) per document. SimHash compresses a document into a 64-bit fingerprint such that textually similar documents land at small Hamming distance from each other. It's computed from a weighted combination of character 4-grams: hash each 4-gram, weight it by its corpus-wide TF-IDF score, and accumulate a 64-bit vector where bit i is the sign of the weighted sum of contributions to bit i across all of a document's 4-grams. Two documents that differ only in minor rewording produce fingerprints with Hamming distance under 3, with high probability.

    Searching a store of billions of fingerprints for anything within Hamming distance 3 of a new one is not a linear scan — it uses permutation-table search: precompute roughly 18 permutation tables, each reordering the 64 bits so that a different subset occupies the leading 48 bits, then binary-search each table for fingerprints sharing the query's leading-48-bit prefix under that permutation. Any true near-duplicate is guaranteed to share a long enough common prefix under at least one of the 18 permutations to be found this way. Across 18 tables and roughly 10B stored fingerprints (the count of documents that survive Stage 1, per Scale Estimation above), this runs in on the order of 100ms — practical at corpus scale in a way a brute-force Hamming-distance scan over billions of entries never would be. SimHash is what catches syndicated news copy with 90%+ textual overlap, low-effort "spun" rewrites from content farms, and Wikipedia forks living at different URLs — none of which share an exact hash with the original, and all of which a search engine can tolerate indexing (a ranking model demotes the weaker copy) in a way an AI corpus should not.

    Stage 3 — semantic deduplication via embedding similarity, the most expensive and highest-precision stage, run only on documents that survived Stages 1 and 2 — a small fraction of total volume, which is what makes its cost acceptable. Embed the document (or a representative excerpt) and compare cosine similarity against recently indexed embeddings; above roughly 0.95, treat it as a near-semantic duplicate even though its surface text differs meaningfully. This catches what surface-level methods structurally cannot: an AI-generated restatement of an original article, a translated copy, or a heavily paraphrased summary — cases where the words differ enough that SimHash's 4-gram fingerprint diverges, but the informational content is the same one fact restated. Embedding one document costs a fraction of a cent; the ANN lookup against a billion-plus prior embeddings adds roughly 10ms — cheap in isolation, but only run as a last-resort filter on documents nothing faster already caught, exactly the same "escalate only what needs it" discipline that governs extraction-tier escalation in Concept 2.

    Cross-domain duplicates get resolved, not merely detected. A single wire-service story often appears verbatim across a hundred syndication domains. Among a cluster of near-duplicates, the copy retained is the one from the highest domain-authority source (Concept 1's domain authority score); the rest are discarded from the output stream but not silently lost — their metadata records a canonical_url pointer to the kept copy, so if the primary source later disappears, the discarded copies remain a recoverable fallback rather than permanently forgotten data.

    Concept 4 — Freshness scheduling¶

    Freshness here means something more general than AI Search Engine's query-serving SLOs: the goal is to concentrate re-crawl budget on content that's actually likely to have changed, and let genuinely static content fall out of the schedule almost entirely, freeing capacity for discovering new URLs rather than repeatedly confirming old ones haven't moved.

    Estimating a per-URL change rate. Every re-crawl compares the new extraction's content hash — the same SHA-256 Stage 1 deduplication computes, reused here rather than recomputed for a second purpose — against the hash stored from the previous crawl: changed (1) or unchanged (0). Over the last N observed crawls, p_change is the fraction that came back changed, smoothed with a Beta-distribution prior so a URL with only three observations doesn't get an overconfident estimate from that thin a sample — its estimate leans toward the domain-level average change rate until enough of its own history accumulates to override it. Re-crawl interval scales inversely with p_change: interval = base_interval / p_change. A page changing 70% of the time the crawler checks gets re-crawled at roughly 1.4× the base interval; a page changing 5% of the time gets re-crawled at 20× the base interval — the mechanism that lets a 100B-URL corpus stay fresh without re-fetching all 100B URLs on the same cadence, which is simply not affordable.

    Tier Content type Freshness target (to downstream consumer) Trigger mechanism
    Tier 0 — Push News, official announcements < 5 minutes WebSub subscription; publisher pushes on publication
    Tier 1 — High-frequency Wikipedia, major documentation sites < 4 hours Sitemap <lastmod> monitoring, RSS polling
    Tier 2 — Standard Technical blogs, reference sites Daily to weekly Adaptive interval from p_change
    Tier 3 — Stable Academic papers, archived content Monthly, or on-demand Long exponential backoff, triggered only by an inlink-velocity spike

    A note on why this table's numbers don't need to match a downstream consumer's SLOs exactly: this crawler's Tier 0 push mechanism can deliver a document to Kafka within minutes of a WebSub notification firing, but a consumer like AI Search Engine, reading from that stream, budgets its own additional time for embedding and index writes on top of that delivery — which is why that chapter's Tier 0 SLO is a looser 15 minutes measured end-to-end at the index, not a contradiction of the 5-minute figure here, but a composition of this system's delivery latency plus that system's own processing latency.

    WebSub for Tier 0. WebSub (formerly PubSubHubbub) is a publish/subscribe standard most major news publishers, and platforms like WordPress.com, already support: the crawler subscribes to a publisher's hub endpoint (discovered from a <link rel="hub"> tag on the publisher's own pages), and the hub pushes a notification to the crawler's subscriber endpoint within seconds of new content going live — no polling loop required at all. On receipt, the notified URL is queued directly into its domain's back queue at the highest priority bucket, bypassing the normal discovery path entirely.

    Sitemaps as ground truth for Tier 1. A sitemap.xml (or a sitemap_index.xml pointing to several) is a publisher's own declaration of <lastmod> per URL. When a sitemap reports a <lastmod> newer than this system's stored last-crawl timestamp for that URL, that's an immediate, elevated-priority re-crawl trigger — the publisher is telling the crawler exactly what changed, which is strictly better ground truth than inferring change from a full re-fetch-and-hash cycle, and cheaper to check on a schedule than re-fetching every tracked URL on that same schedule.

    Adaptive backoff for Tier 3. A URL with p_change below roughly 0.1 starts at a weekly re-crawl interval; each unchanged re-crawl doubles the interval (bi-weekly, then monthly), capped at 90 days for the most stable content observed. A single change after a long stable stretch resets the interval back to weekly — the assumption that "this page is now static" is cheap to hold and cheap to revise.

    Externally-triggered re-crawl. A sudden spike in inbound-link velocity, a mention on a major aggregator, or appearance in a news-ranking feed is a signal a URL changed before this system's own change-rate model has had a chance to observe it directly. Any of these trigger an immediate out-of-schedule re-crawl, regardless of the URL's current tier or computed next_crawl_time — a mechanism for reacting to signals the internal change-rate estimator, by construction, always lags.

    Concept 5 — The clean-to-indexed handoff pipeline¶

    The crawler's output is not an end in itself — it exists to be consumed, and the interface it exposes matters as much as anything internal to this system.

    Output document schema. Every surviving document produces one structured record:

    {
  "url": "https://example.com/article",
  "canonical_url": "https://example.com/article",
  "crawled_at": "2026-07-01T12:00:00Z",
  "content_hash": "sha256:abc123...",
  "extraction_tier": 1,
  "extraction_confidence": 0.91,
  "title": "Article Title",
  "author": "Jane Doe",
  "published_at": "2026-06-28T09:00:00Z",
  "language": "en",
  "content_type": "article",
  "domain_tier": 1,
  "domain_authority": 0.82,
  "word_count": 1240,
  "body_markdown": "# Section 1\n\nParagraph text...\n\n## Section 2\n...",
  "structured_data": {"schema_org_type": "NewsArticle"},
  "change_rate_estimate": 0.35,
  "outlinks": ["https://related.com/page"]
}

    content_type and domain_tier are what let a downstream chunking-strategy selector make an informed choice without re-deriving anything: documentation pages route to structure-aware, heading-boundary chunking; news articles route to semantic, paragraph-boundary chunking; forum threads and comment pages are excluded outright as high-noise, low-density content unfit for an AI corpus regardless of how they'd chunk. body_markdown is the artifact everything else exists to produce — structured enough for a chunker to use heading levels as natural split points, clean enough that an embedding model never sees a navigation menu it might otherwise represent as if it were content.

    Handoff via a durable stream, not a direct call. Completed records publish to a Kafka topic (crawled-documents); RAG System's ingestion pipeline subscribes to it. This decoupling buys three things a direct call wouldn't: backpressure absorption (a slower ingestion pipeline simply lets documents buffer in Kafka rather than stalling the crawl fleet), replay (a bug discovered in the ingestion pipeline is fixed by re-processing from an earlier offset, not by re-crawling), and independent scaling (the crawl fleet and the ingestion fleet scale on their own schedules, driven by their own bottlenecks).

    Crawl status as the freshness source of truth. A separate store — keyed by canonical_url, holding last_crawled_at, content_hash, extraction_confidence, domain_tier, change_rate_estimate, and next_scheduled_crawl — is what Concept 4's scheduling reads from and writes back to. At 100B URLs and roughly 200 bytes of metadata each, that's the ~20TB figure from Capacity Planning, held in a distributed key-value store (Cassandra- or DynamoDB-class) with a TTL that expires entries for URLs no inlink has referenced in over a year — the frontier's memory of a URL is not meant to be permanent for content nobody links to anymore.

    API Design¶

    Three surfaces: two internal, used by the pipeline stages themselves, and one operational, used by the humans running the system.

    POST /frontier/enqueue
{ "url": "https://example.com/article", "priority_score": 0.62, "source_domain": "referrer.com" }
→ Enqueues after canonicalization, Bloom-filter dedup check, and robots.txt authorization;
  a URL failing any of those never reaches a back queue.

GET /frontier/next?domain=example.com
→ Returns the next URL for that domain if its crawl delay has expired; empty otherwise.

POST /extract
{ "raw_html": "...", "url": "https://example.com/article", "domain_tier": 1 }
→ Returns a clean document record (the schema above), tier-escalating internally as needed.

POST /domains/block
{ "domain": "disputed-source.com", "reason": "legal takedown request" }
→ Halts all crawling of the domain immediately; existing frontier entries for it are purged.

GET /domains/{domain}/status
→ Returns queue depth, last-fetch time, effective crawl delay, and a robots.txt summary for the domain.

POST /urls/prioritize
{ "url": "https://news-source.com/breaking-story" }
→ Forces an immediate re-crawl at the highest priority bucket, bypassing the normal schedule —
  the mechanism the news desk uses when a breaking story needs ingestion before its next scheduled crawl.

    /frontier/enqueue and /frontier/next are the internal contract between outlink discovery and the scheduler — never called by anything outside the crawl fleet itself. /domains/block and /urls/prioritize are the two operational levers with real consequences: the first is how a legal or compliance team stops the crawler from touching a domain immediately, the second is how time-sensitive content skips the queue without weakening politeness or priority scoring for everything else.

    Retrieval Layer¶

    This system has no query-serving retrieval path in the traditional sense — it's a write-heavy pipeline, not something users query. The only lookup structures in its hot path are the two dedup stores from Concepts 1 and 3, and they're worth sizing explicitly because they're where this system's own latency budget actually goes:

    • Bloom filter (seen-URL check): the 1.4TB structure from Capacity Planning, sharded via consistent hashing across a Redis cluster — a realistic layout spreads it across roughly 16 nodes at ~90GB each, giving every canonicalized URL an O(1), single-digit-millisecond membership check before it's allowed anywhere near a back queue.
    • SimHash permutation tables: 18 tables over the ~10B fingerprints that survive exact dedup (Scale Estimation above), ~1.4TB total — colocated with the Bloom filter cluster operationally, since both are hot, memory-resident, high-QPS structures with the same operational profile (no durability requirement beyond periodic snapshotting; a lost node's data is cheaply rebuilt from the crawl status store rather than treated as a disaster-recovery event).

    Both structures share a design property worth naming: neither one is the source of truth for anything. The Bloom filter can false-positive; the crawl status store (Concept 5) and the underlying content are the durable record. This is why both can be sharded aggressively for speed without a matching investment in durability — losing a shard temporarily degrades dedup precision, not correctness of the underlying corpus.

    Agent Layer¶

    This system is deliberately not agentic, and it's worth being explicit about why, given how much of its behavior — tier escalation in extraction, adaptive re-crawl intervals, priority scoring — could be described in agent-shaped language if described loosely. Every one of those behaviors is a fixed function of a measured signal, evaluated identically every time: extraction escalates to Tier 2 when a confidence score crosses a threshold, not because anything "decided" the page deserved more scrutiny; a URL's re-crawl interval shrinks when p_change rises, computed the same way for every URL in the corpus. There is no planner reasoning about what to fetch next, no reflection step second-guessing an extraction result, no loop deciding whether to gather more evidence before publishing a document. That's a real architectural boundary, not a simplification for this chapter's sake: Perplexity's live-fetch path and Deep Research Agent's iterative retrieval loop both involve an LLM reasoning, turn by turn, about which URL to fetch next based on what it's already found — genuinely agentic fetch behavior. This system's frontier does the opposite on purpose: the same URL, discovered the same way, produces the same priority score and the same tier-escalation path regardless of what any other URL's crawl turned up, because a crawl fleet running at 10,000 pages/second needs decisions that are cheap, deterministic, and auditable — not decisions that require an LLM call per URL.

    The two components in Model Layer below that look the most "learned" — the domain authority scorer and the content type classifier — are also not agents: they're static classifiers, scored once per input and left alone, feeding numbers into the fixed priority formula in Concept 1 rather than making decisions of their own.

    Model Layer¶

    Three lightweight ML components sit inside the crawler, each doing one narrow job rather than any single model trying to do all three:

    Model Job Update cadence Why this cadence
    Domain authority scorer Graph-based, PageRank-style score over the domain link graph Weekly batch The link graph shifts slowly enough that daily recomputation buys nothing; a week is fast enough to catch a genuinely rising domain
    Content type classifier Categorizes a page as article / documentation / reference / forum / archive / other, from title plus first ~200 words Monthly, or on drift signal Retraining is cheap (a small classifier, not a foundation model) but unnecessary more often than page-type distributions actually shift
    Language detector fastText language ID, under 2MB, CPU-only Rarely — the model itself is stable across long periods Language identification is a solved, low-drift problem; the model exists to filter unsupported languages before ingestion, not to track a moving target

    None of these three feed a generation step — they feed the priority formula (Concept 1) and the chunking-strategy selector downstream (Concept 5). That's a deliberate contrast with every other Model Layer section in this book: there is no LLM anywhere in this system's hot path, and the throughput numbers in Capacity Planning — 10,000 pages/second — would not be achievable at any reasonable cost if there were.

    Security Layer¶

    Four concerns get more architectural weight here than in any other chapter in this book, because here they're hard technical gates, not policy documents that happen to exist alongside the system.

    robots.txt compliance is structurally enforced, not merely observed. A URL cannot be admitted to any back queue without a confirmed, cached authorization from that domain's robots.txt (Concept 1) — compliance isn't a check the crawler happens to run, it's a precondition the frontier's admission logic makes impossible to bypass. Crawl-delay directives are read and enforced as the domain's effective politeness interval, overriding the 1-second default whenever present.

    Rate limiting extends beyond what robots.txt requires. Even a domain with no Crawl-delay directive gets the default 1-request-per-second floor, enforced centrally at the scheduler (Concept 1) — never left to individual fetcher workers' discretion, since uncoordinated per-worker rate limiting on a shared domain silently multiplies into an aggregate rate no single worker sees. Beyond per-domain limits, aggregate request rate is also watched at the IP level: a fetcher crawling several domains that happen to share infrastructure (a common CDN, the same hosting provider) must not let its combined request rate across those domains exceed what that shared infrastructure can absorb without mistaking the crawler for an attack.

    Terms-of-service compliance is a process the architecture supports, not one it can fully automate. robots.txt is a machine-readable, structural standard; a site's terms of service is unstructured prose a crawler cannot parse and enforce on its own — a domain can permit all crawlers in its robots.txt while its ToS separately prohibits use of that content for AI training, and no automated check bridges that gap. The architecture's answer is a legal-review-backed domain blocklist, operationalized through POST /domains/block (API Design above), plus a dispute-intake process for domain operators to request removal. High-sensitivity corpora (legal, medical, anything with elevated compliance exposure) require legal review before a new domain is promoted to Tier 0 or Tier 1 crawl priority at all, rather than being discovered and prioritized purely by the automated signals in Concept 1.

    User-Agent identification is mandatory, not optional. Every fetch identifies the crawler with a descriptive User-Agent (OrganizationBot/1.0; +https://organization.com/bot-info) so a webmaster can identify the traffic, understand its purpose, and add a targeted Disallow if they choose to — the opposite of the evasive, unidentifiable traffic pattern a hostile scraper produces, and part of what keeps this system's traffic distinguishable from an attack in the first place.

    Cost Model¶

    Component Basis Approximate daily cost
    Fetcher fleet 33 machines (Capacity Planning) ~$120/day
    Extraction fleet (Tier 1/2) 20 machines ~$145/day
    Headless browser pool (Tier 3) Sized for the 5% JavaScript-rendered slice at ~500ms/page ~$155/day
    Bloom filter + SimHash cluster ~2.8TB combined, memory-optimized nodes ~$530/day
    Crawl status store ~20TB distributed KV ~$170/day
    Kafka (crawled-documents) 3-broker cluster, ~50MB/s ~$35/day
    Crawl infrastructure total ~$1,150/day

    Against that roughly \(1,150/day** to run the entire acquisition layer, Scale Estimation's downstream embedding-cost ceiling — up to **\)86,400/day if every fetched page were re-embedded on every crawl — is close to a 75× multiplier. Smaller than AI Search Engine's 900× synthesis-versus-retrieval gap, but the same qualitative shape, and the same lesson Cost Engineering generalizes: acquisition is cheap; what an AI system does with what it acquires is where the money goes. In practice the realized embedding bill sits well below that ceiling, because change detection (Concept 4) means only pages whose content-hash actually moved get re-embedded — which makes the single highest-leverage cost lever in this entire system a crawler-side one: a well-tuned change-rate estimator that avoids wasted re-crawls saves money in a downstream pipeline this system doesn't even operate.

    Within the crawl infrastructure's own bill, two levers matter most: the Tier 3 escalation threshold (Concept 2) — escalating to headless rendering more readily buys extraction accuracy at a real, measurable per-page cost premium (roughly 10× a Tier 1 extraction), so tightening that threshold is a direct cost dial — and priority-score tuning (Concept 1), where over-weighting broad discovery over content-quality signal inflates the fetcher and extraction fleets' workload with thin, low-value pages that were never going to earn their crawl cost back downstream.

    Failure Handling¶

    Failure Degradation strategy
    Fetcher pool saturated with Tier 0 backlog Redirect idle or Tier 2/3 capacity into a burst pool for Tier 0 until the backlog clears, rather than silently missing the freshness SLO
    robots.txt fetch fails (timeout or 5xx) Retry with backoff; do not admit any URL on that domain to a back queue until a robots.txt fetch succeeds — crawling without a confirmed policy is not an acceptable failure mode
    A domain returns repeated 429s Automatically extend that domain's effective crawl delay beyond its declared value, and alert if the pattern persists — a signal the domain's actual tolerance is lower than what it advertised
    Bloom filter cluster degraded or unavailable Fail open: admit URLs without the dedup check, tagging them for a reconciliation pass once the cluster recovers — failing closed would halt all new-URL discovery fleet-wide, a far larger cost than a temporary rise in duplicate admissions
    SimHash index unavailable Skip near-duplicate detection temporarily; exact-hash dedup (Stage 1) still applies as a backstop, and affected documents are flagged for a later reprocessing pass
    Headless browser pool exhausted Queue the Tier 3 candidate and serve its lower-confidence Tier 1 or Tier 2 result immediately, tagged with the lower extraction_confidence rather than blocking on browser availability
    WebSub subscription silently expires for a publisher Fall back to sitemap polling for that publisher at Tier 1 cadence until a subscription renewal succeeds again
    Kafka consumer lag grows on crawled-documents Throttle crawl-fleet dispatch rate to match downstream consumption rather than dropping documents — the durable log absorbs a temporary backlog safely as long as producers slow to match it
    Crawl status store unavailable Fall back to a best-effort in-memory cache of recent crawl timestamps; new-URL discovery continues, but freshness-scheduling precision degrades until the store recovers

    Tradeoff Analysis¶

    flowchart TD
    Q1{"Priority scoring weight?"} -->|"Favor content quality"| QUALITY["Higher corpus quality, misses long-tail pages"]
    Q1 -->|"Favor broad coverage"| COVERAGE["Broader discovery, more noise to filter downstream"]
    Q2{"Extraction escalation threshold?"} -->|"Escalate aggressively"| ACCURATE["Higher extraction accuracy, higher cost and latency"]
    Q2 -->|"Escalate rarely"| FAST["Lower cost, more borderline extractions accepted"]
    Q3{"SimHash Hamming threshold?"} -->|"Tighter - distance 1 to 2"| PRECISE["Fewer false-positive duplicate drops, misses more true duplicates"]
    Q3 -->|"Looser - distance 3 to 4"| RECALL["Catches more true duplicates, risks dropping distinct short pages"]
    1. Crawl coverage vs. content quality. Following every outlink maximizes URL discovery but dilutes the corpus with thin, noisy pages; following only high-content-density links yields a cleaner corpus at the cost of missing valuable long-tail content. Tuned through the content-quality weight in Concept 1's priority formula — there is no setting that's simply "correct," only a setting matched to how much the downstream consumer values breadth over density.
    2. Extraction escalation threshold. A low bar for escalating from Tier 1 to Tier 2 (or Tier 2 to Tier 3) improves extraction quality but adds real cost and latency to a larger share of pages; a high bar keeps more traffic on the fast, cheap path but accepts worse extraction on borderline pages that don't quite trip the threshold. Calibrated against a labeled evaluation set of extraction confidence versus ground-truth content region, not chosen by intuition.
    3. SimHash deduplication threshold. Hamming distance ≤ 3 catches near-duplicates effectively but risks the occasional false positive — two genuinely distinct short articles on the same narrow topic can coincidentally land within that distance. Tightening the threshold trades recall on true duplicates for fewer wrongful drops; the right setting depends on how costly a wrongly-dropped distinct document is relative to a duplicate that slips through.
    4. Politeness interval. A shorter crawl delay improves freshness at the cost of higher load on target servers and real risk of IP bans; a longer delay is safer but widens freshness lag. The default of 1 second (or the domain's own declared Crawl-delay, whichever is stricter) is not a starting point to be tuned down under freshness pressure — Security Layer above treats this as a floor, not a dial.
    5. Headless rendering scope. Rendering every page headlessly would extract JavaScript-heavy content perfectly, at roughly 10× the cost and latency of the fast path across the entire corpus; rendering nothing headlessly misses an entire class of modern sites outright. The tiered escalation in Concept 2 — render only the pages Tier 1 and Tier 2 flag as likely JavaScript-dependent — is the pragmatic middle ground, and the reason Tier 3 gets its own small, separately-sized pool rather than being folded into general extraction capacity.

    Interview Discussion¶

    "Design a web crawler for AI knowledge ingestion" tests something a generic "design a web crawler" prompt doesn't. The classic version tests URL deduplication at scale (a Bloom filter), distributed frontier management, DNS caching, and politeness — all still present here, all still necessary. The AI-specific variant layers on four things a candidate has to name to actually be answering the question asked: content extraction quality (not just fetching a page, but precisely deciding what to keep from it), near-duplicate detection at a semantic level (not just URL-level dedup), a structured output format (Markdown with metadata, not a raw text dump), and a freshness architecture built for a corpus that must stay current rather than a one-time crawl.

    A weak answer says "BFS from seed URLs, store in Kafka, use Scrapy for extraction" — the tools are reasonable, but there's no architectural depth on the quality problem that's actually the point of the AI-specific framing. A strong answer explains the back-queue politeness mechanism concretely (not "rate limit per domain" as a phrase, but the front-queue/back-queue split and why a single priority queue can't enforce politeness on its own), describes the tiered extraction pipeline with confidence-based escalation, treats near-duplicate detection as the three-stage problem it actually is (exact hash → SimHash → semantic embedding), and can say specifically why the output is structured Markdown rather than plain text — because the chunker downstream depends on it.

    Staff-level probe: "Your Tier 0 news-crawl latency to the downstream consumer is suddenly 25 minutes instead of under 5, for 30% of sources. What's happening, and how do you diagnose it?" Three candidate causes, in the order worth checking: (1) WebSub subscriptions for those specific publishers have silently expired — a hub-side renewal handshake failed and push delivery quietly stopped, with sitemap polling (the fallback) running at Tier 1's slower cadence instead; (2) the fetcher pool is saturated with lower-priority traffic and Tier 0 URLs are queued behind it rather than being dispatched immediately; (3) the affected publishers' own servers have gotten slower, and the crawler's own request rate is being throttled server-side. Diagnosis order: check WebSub subscription health per affected publisher first (are renewal handshakes actually succeeding, or silently failing), then Tier 0 queue depth specifically (should be near-empty by design; a backlog points at fetcher capacity), then per-domain fetch latency from the fetcher's own metrics (distinguishing "the server is slow" from "our queue wait is slow" — two different fixes).

    Staff-level probe: "Legal finds a corpus document from a domain whose robots.txt allows all crawlers, but whose terms of service explicitly prohibits crawling for AI training. What's the architectural response?" robots.txt compliance is the technical standard this system enforces mechanically; a ToS is a legal constraint no crawler can parse or enforce automatically, because it's unstructured prose, not a machine-readable directive. The immediate action is operational, not architectural: block the domain via POST /domains/block and purge its frontier entries. The durable fix is process, not code: a legal-review-backed domain blocklist that can be updated ahead of discovery for known-sensitive domains, a dispute-intake channel for domain operators to request removal, and — for corpora with real compliance exposure (legal, medical) — a requirement that legal review a domain before it's promoted to high-priority crawl tiers at all, rather than being admitted purely on the automated signals in Concept 1. Naming this honestly as a process gap the architecture supports but cannot fully close on its own is the answer a staff-level candidate gives; pretending a technical filter can fully substitute for legal review is the answer that doesn't hold up under follow-up.
